# Stein & Pollmann (eds.) — Networks, Regions and Nations (2010)

Robert Stein and Judith Pollmann (eds.), *Networks, Regions and Nations: Shaping Identities in the Low Countries, 1300–1650* (Brill, 2010).

## Source-derived findings relevant to Claes

### Composite identities rather than one national identity
Mid-sixteenth-century Low Countries were a composite polity with strong local and regional identities, different jurisdictions, languages and traditions. Supra-regional Netherlandish identity developed alongside rather than simply replacing town/province identities.

### Identity as interaction, not top-down imposition
The volume stresses interaction between rulers, urban elites and subjects. Habsburg public ritual, entries, processions, shooting guilds and chambers of rhetoric created shared messages but could simultaneously reinforce localism.

### Public performance as political communication
Princely entries, tableaux vivants, oaths, bells, processions, plays and competitions were communicative acts. Meanings could be legible differently to elites and ordinary spectators. Patronage of chambers of rhetoric and shooting guilds linked court culture with urban cultural networks.

### Rederijkers and shooting guilds as identity infrastructures
Van Bruaene's contribution shows that Habsburg rulers used support for large interregional shooting and rhetoric competitions to communicate with urban groups. Chambers were less socially exclusive than shooting guilds and vernacular theatre helped articulate linguistic and territorial identities.

### War creates in-groups and enemies
The volume's synthesis emphasizes that war shapes identity by forcing questions of who 'we' are and who the enemy is. Urban defeat, sack, humiliation and ritual subjection could paradoxically intensify civic identity.

### 1555–1576: patriotism, liberty and anti-Spanish language
Duke shows that criticism of royal anti-heresy policy, fear of 'inquisition', urban liberties and complaints that Spanish interests were placed above those of the Low Countries contributed to a growing concept of a common political community. This does not mean a modern national identity already existed.

### Pollmann — No Man's Land, 1585–1621
Pollmann argues that after 1585 older Netherlandish unity language persisted but became contested. Propaganda increasingly used identity labels to 'other' fellow Netherlanders. Northern and Southern propagandists could both claim Netherlandish legitimacy while casting opponents as alienated, Hispanicised, heretical, rebellious or deceived.

For late Claes this means that a man formed in Antwerp, Zeeland and northern cities can inhabit overlapping identities that later political borders try to force apart. Exiles were especially prone both to preserve memories of a shared Netherlands and to articulate the growing North/South difference.

## Narrative use
- Never make Claes think in a modern Netherlands/Belgium binary.
- Let identity be layered: town, province, craft/chamber, confession, dynasty, 'fatherland', language and personal network.
- Public ritual should communicate simultaneously through sight, sound, heraldry, costume, oath, procession and performance.
- After 1585, words such as Netherlander, Hollander, Spaniard, patriot and Catholic/Reformed can become strategic labels rather than transparent demographic descriptions.
- Migration and exile can preserve old belonging while sharpening new difference.

## Transfer guardrail
Evidence drawn from Antwerp/Brabant/Holland or supra-regional synthesis supplies mechanisms, not exact Goes/Reimerswaal practice. Local identities and civic rituals remain locally sourced.
