## Officially verified scope and argument
Oxford Academic and Leiden University describe the study as an investigation of how Netherlandish Catholics experienced the religious and political upheavals of the Revolt, using diaries, memoirs, poems, plays and pamphlets to foreground individual Catholic voices rather than only institutional records.

The official table of contents establishes these chronological units:
1. *A pious people* — including 1566 and pre-Reformation Catholic life;
2. *Each should tend his own garden: Strategies, 1520–1566*;
3. *Retribution and reform, 1566–1571*;
4. *‘Catholics were not asked’: Rebellion, 1572–1585*;
5. *Reconciliation and atonement, 1585–1598*;
6. *Marshalling the sacred, 1598–1621*;
7. Epilogue: Tilburg, 1633.

Official chapter abstracts support two important guardrails for Claes:
- early-sixteenth-century Catholic laypeople could be deeply attached to and knowledgeable about their faith; apparent passivity in the early Reformation cannot simply be equated with indifference;
- Protestant reform emerged in part from developments within existing Christian culture, making confessional responses initially complex rather than instantly binary.
