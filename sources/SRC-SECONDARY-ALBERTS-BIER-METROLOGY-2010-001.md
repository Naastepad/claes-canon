# SRC-SECONDARY-ALBERTS-BIER-METROLOGY-2010-001 — Beer-barrel metrology

- **Source ID:** `SRC-SECONDARY-ALBERTS-BIER-METROLOGY-2010-001`
- **Source type:** scholarly metrological study
- **Author:** Leen Alberts
- **Work:** `Bier drinken met maten. Dronkenschap en het alcoholgehalte van Stichtse en Hollandse bieren in de late middeleeuwen`, *Jaarboek voor Middeleeuwse Geschiedenis* 13 (2010), 113–166
- **Consulted representation:** publicly indexed article text
- **Verification date:** 2026-08-30
- **Use in Claes:** order-of-magnitude and unit guardrails for beer-cask scenes; never a universal local-volume converter.

## Holland beer ton around 1590

Alberts reports that the merchant handbook *Tresoir* of Herman Jansz Muller (1590) gives the **Hollandse bierton** as 54 Antwerp stopen, approximately **154 litres**.

## Delft variation

The same study explicitly warns against assuming one uniform Holland barrel: in **1578 the Delft beer ton was smaller than the Amsterdam beer ton**, even though the Delft measure served as a standard in several places.

## Claes use

For a late-sixteenth-century cargo described only as `tonnen bier`, 154 L is a useful **Holland-scale proxy**, not an automatic Delft or Goes conversion.

Thus a documented consignment of 52 beer tons can be visualized as being on the order of several thousand litres; using 154 L mechanically would yield about 8,008 L, but that figure is a derived proxy, **not** the recovered volume of the 1589 Goese cargo and not De Hont's capacity.

## Guardrails

1. Preserve the historical unit first; normalize only secondarily.
2. Do not assign every Delft beer ton exactly 154 L.
3. Do not equate vessel `last` with beer-trade `last` without a source-specific bridge.
4. Do not use the 154 L proxy to calculate an exact vessel capacity for Aernouldt de Hont.
