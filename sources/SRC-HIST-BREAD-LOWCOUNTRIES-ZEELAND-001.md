# SRC-HIST-BREAD-LOWCOUNTRIES-ZEELAND-001 — bread, grain and baking practice

**Type:** historical material-culture source bundle  
**Status:** TARGETED_REVIEW / TRANSFERABLE_WITH_LOCAL_LIMITS  
**Scope:** urban bread production, grain hierarchy, bread assize and sensory craft practice relevant to Goes, Antwerp and later North-Holland scenes, ca. 1500–1602.

## Supported working model

For sixteenth-century urban scenes, the safest default is a professional or regulated bakery rather than an invented private household oven. Bread production joins grain supply, milling/bolting, dough preparation, fermentation, oven management, cooling, weighing and sale. Exact recipes, temperatures, fermentation times, loaf weights and Goese bakery locations are not established by this bundle.

Broad Low Countries evidence supports social and economic differentiation among grain/flour qualities and the long-running use of bread assizes: authorities could regulate the relation between grain price and loaf price/weight. A 1622 Zeeland example is useful as continuity evidence, not as an exact 1550 Goes tariff.

The practical chain used for scene construction is therefore:

`grain supply → milling/bolting → flour → water/salt/ferment → mixing/kneading → fermentation → shaping → firing/heating oven → clearing/preparing oven → baking → cooling/inspection → weighing/sale/consumption`

This is a transferable craft sequence, not a reconstructed Goese recipe.

## Sensory use

Historically defensible sensory cues include flour dust, texture and resistance of dough, fermentation smell, wood smoke, radiant oven heat, crust colour/firmness, loaf weight and crumb condition. These should be used diagnostically — a worker notices whether matter behaves as expected — rather than as a decorative five-senses checklist.

## Evidence boundaries

- Wheat, rye and mixed-grain breads all belong to the wider Low Countries bread economy, but exact Goese 1550 consumption shares are UNKNOWN.
- `Masteluin` is a historically relevant mixed-grain category, but a scene must not assume one fixed proportion or social meaning without a local source.
- Brewer's yeast/`gest` as baking ferment is a plausible early-modern craft connection; this bundle does not prove a specific Goese baker used it on a specified day in the 1540s/1550s.
- Scarcity could push poorer consumers toward inferior/substitute grains; a 1522 Zeeland report of expensive wheat and bread from barley/peas/beans is scarcity evidence, not normal daily bread composition.
- Later Goese regulations are useful for institutional continuity only and must not be projected backwards as exact sixteenth-century ordinances.

## Writing guardrails

1. Do not invent an exact Goese recipe, oven temperature, proofing time or loaf weight.
2. Do not make every household possess its own oven.
3. Do not present white wheat/rye/masteluin as a rigid modern class taxonomy.
4. Do not use later quantitative consumption figures for Claes' childhood.
5. Let craft judgement arise through handling matter: touch, resistance, smell, heat, colour and weight.
6. A youth bakery scene remains an authorial scene choice; this source bundle supplies world practice, not Claes' presence.
