## 1. Governing proposition

**Controlling idea — `THEME.CLAES.CONTROLLING_IDEA`**  
A man who spends his life seeking hidden order becomes whole only when knowledge ceases to be a means of control and becomes responsibility toward other people.

**Dramatic question — `THEME.CLAES.DRAMATIC_QUESTION`**  
What does truth ask of Claes toward the other when certainty, safety and control are impossible?

**Character engine**
- Desire: understand the hidden order behind visible reality.
- Psychological need: recover trust in embodied perception — the *sinne* — and act without complete certainty.
- Moral need: understand that knowledge and perception increase responsibility toward the other.
- Gift: prolonged, exact, embodied observation.
- Shadow: continuing to observe after action is required.
- Child lie: if I look carefully enough, I can prevent surprise or loss.
- Adult lie: if I understand the process, I can control the consequences.
- Final truth: right action does not require certainty of success; mature mastery can transmit and release rather than possess.

Canonical anchors: `STC.CLAES.SINNE.001`, `STC.CLAES.PARADOX.001`, `STC.CLAES.NEED.001`, `STC.CLAES.MORAL_QUESTION.001` and the decisions of 13–14 August 2026.

## 4. Claes — cradle to grave and the *sinne*

### I — 1542–ca. 1547: body before meaning
The world enters Claes through warmth, cold, smell, light, sound, silence, rhythm, food, wax, incense and touch before language. The body knows before the mind explains.

### II — ca. 1547–1554: discovering order
Home, church, school, brewing, trade and merels teach relation and process. The *sinne* develops through sensation → recognition → comparison → pattern → understanding. Cornelis trains attention through waiting, smelling, counting and looking again.

### III — 1554–1561: first blackening
The Goes fire, displacement to Reimerswaal, water threat and later losses change open perception into vigilance. Claes learns that seeing accurately does not prevent change or loss. The wound begins: *I did not see early enough what was happening.*

### IV — 1560–1564: disciplined interpretation
As older pupil, then in Antwerp, perception becomes analytic and practical. Landjuweel, print culture, copying, commerce, Silvius and Dee teach that carrier, role, intention and effect can diverge. Claes moves from observer to causal actor.

### V — 4 Oct 1564–1578: agency under consequence
Secrecy, compartmentalisation, Cornelis' fall and the 1570 recovery architecture intensify the adult lie that process can be controlled. The code is solved only through weeks of work and collaboration; knowledge becomes moral burden rather than private possession.

### VI — 1578–1584: sensory and moral constriction
Accumulated trauma, secrecy, consequence and responsibility blunt or constrict the *sinne*. The wound changes into: *I saw and still did not do enough.* Delft 1584 is the moral bottom.

### VII — 1584–1602+: recovery, resonance, sovereignty
On the road toward Enkhuizen, with his beloved beside him — the apothecary's daughter whose identity and full biography remain open — Claes rediscovers embodied perception. She accompanies and catalyses the process but does not perform it for him. Sensation begins to echo and resonate within the self rather than merely registering danger outside it.

The recovered *sinne* is not a return to childhood innocence. It becomes a deeper sequence:

**perceive → distinguish → choose → carry → release**

This is simultaneously trauma recovery, moral maturation and the lived Great Work. Claes grows toward wisdom and inner sovereignty as an alchemist not because he escapes matter, but because he learns to read and transform his relation to matter, body, memory and the other.

## 11. World modules and historical discipline

Goes, Reimerswaal, Antwerp, Delft, Middelburg and Enkhuizen remain source-weighted historical environments. Distinguish historical anchor, reconstruction and fiction. Do not manufacture exact topography, school personnel, institutional structures, trade practices or religious affiliations where sources do not support them.

### Goes — living city, property network and routes

`WORLD.GOES_LIVING_CITY` is the operating spatial/social framework for Goes scenes. Its evidence bundle is `SRC-HIST-GOES-LIVING-CITY-001` with normalized `SC.HIST.GOES.*` claims and addressable entities for market, harbour, salt-industry zones, gates, school, streets, Nissepad, family properties and event footprints. The dense Stadsatlas/RAZE layer remains the geometric and register-level companion: the Storybible stores meaning, time, relation and certainty; the atlas stores parcel topology, route geometry and the large register population.

A Goes scene must therefore resolve **year before place** and **relation before inference**. `RESIDES`, `OWNS`, `RENTS`, `OPERATES_BUSINESS`, `WORKS_AT`, `ADJOINS`, `USES` and `VISITS` are separate relations. The general rule remains that ownership or adjacency does not prove residence. For Cornelis' household, however, the author has now made the missing story choice explicitly (`DEC.CORNELIS.RESIDENCE.GOES.2026-08-14`): the house bought by historical Claes Jacobsz. Nissepat on **20 March 1542** in the older Nieuwstraat becomes the fictional family home of Cornelis, his wife and young Claes. The purchase and deed topology are historical; household occupation is novel canon. This home is not automatically Cornelis' brewery, warehouse, salt site or Nissepad business location. The atlas route to church, market, school, Zusterhuis and business must henceforth start from this resolved origin.

The city is to be written as inhabited, not as empty geometry. The normalized transport corpus contains 9,912 acts with named buyers, sellers, neighbouring people, properties, streets and four belendingen per act. These names and occupational labels may be queried by year and street/zone to populate the background: smiths, wagon-makers, ship-related craftsmen, bakers, priests, organists and other attested trades can become the people Claes passes or hears about, provided a named historical person is not silently promoted from property-network presence to proven residence or encounter. Generic figures may be used as generic figures; named historical residents require the appropriate evidence level.

The principal scene anchors are the Grote Markt as civic/market focus; the Maria Magdalenakerk; the harbour and inner harbour; Westzelke and Oostzelke as salt-industry edges; Turfkade; the Kruisbroeders complex; the Ganzepoort/Zuidpoort and Voorstad; the western 's-Heer Hendrikskinderenpoort; the harbour gate/access; the Zusterhuis/Singelstraat as Cornelis' resolved rederijker meeting environment; and the southern route through Voorstad toward Karnemelkseput and the Nissepad corridor. The Nissepad proper must not be stretched all the way to the city gate: in the route reconstruction it begins beyond the Karnemelkseput. The normal south-to-city logic is therefore **Nissepad corridor → Karnemelkseput → Voorstad → Ganzepoort → inner city**, with exact inner-city streets supplied by the year-valid atlas route.

The school is likewise time-sensitive. A 17 January 1558 transport act places a house *bij de stadsschool in het Schuttershofstraatje*, which establishes a school anchor by that date. It does not by itself prove that the school occupied the identical site in 1542, nor that Claes' early schooling was already a formal Latin-school curriculum. Scenes before 1558 must therefore distinguish the existence of schooling from certainty about that later attested micro-location.

The **Nieuwstraat identity is resolved at the level the evidence permits** (`DEC.GOES.NIEUWSTRAAT.IDENTITY.2026-08-14`). The planmatige/current Nieuwstraat belongs to the city expansion decided in **1594**. That does not erase the earlier transport evidence: deeds already use the name `Nieuwstraat` before 1594, including Claes Jacobsz. Nissepat's purchase of 20 March 1542. Later transport locators explicitly connect `Nieuwstraat`/`Oude Nieuwstraat` with the **Armenhoek**. The operating reconstruction therefore treats the pre-1594 name as an older Nieuwstraat/Oude Nieuwstraat in or by the Armenhoek, completely distinct from the 1594 street. Its exact 1542 axis remains unknown. For the 1542 house the deed itself controls the micro-topology: street east, Jacob Dierixsen de Bye north, west and south, so the property lies on the west side of that historical street. No modern house number or exact cadastral line is invented.

The historical Nissepat layer is a property network rather than a proven single genealogy. Claes Jacobsz. Nissepat sold a Noordeinde house beside the Kruisbroeders in 1534; in 1540 he sold house and land by the Nissepad while remaining the eastern neighbour; in March 1542 he bought the older-Nieuwstraat house now selected as Cornelis' fictional household residence. Later Nissepat records include Claes Piersen at Noordeinde in 1568, Jan Jansen in Westzelke/Vismarkt transactions around 1577–1579 and Jan Pauwelsen as neighbour of the documented Nissepad brewery in 1577. These records make family proximity, property movement and social texture available for fiction, but they do **not** prove that all named Nissepat persons belong to one reconstructed nuclear family. The fictional grandfather link from Claes Jacobsz. to Cornelis remains a story hypothesis/choice unless separately canonised.

The **18 May 1554 fire** must be read from the harbour inward. The relevant salt-work complex lay **north of the walled city along the harbour**, not south of Goes. Oostzelke and Westzelke are therefore harbour-industrial edges. The specialized local reconstruction gives **81 salt pans/keten in the harbour zone**, but that number is the total industrial inventory and must never be converted into “81 keten burned”. The documented origin remains one unidentified Oostzelke keten. A strong north-easterly wind then carried sparks and destruction broadly west and south-west.

The source-supported fire corridor is best expressed as **Oostzelke origin → neighbouring harbour/salt structures → wind-driven sparks and multiple secondary harbour/city ignitions → Turfkade and north-western harbour/city fabric → watermill/Molenwater → Kruisbroeders/western fabric**. This is not a claim that every node ignited neatly after the previous node. Once several structures are burning, modern fire-science analogues make a **brandwaaier / spotting pattern** physically credible: a roof or outbuilding can begin smoking ahead of the main flame field while intermediate buildings are temporarily intact. That firebrand mechanism is reconstruction support only; it does not supply a sixteenth-century Goese wind speed, ember range, roof material or exact ignition minute. Roughly one quarter of city/harbour building stock remains the current source-weighted scale. The Opril may be used as a later damage/rebuilding marker, not as a meter-exact terminal fire line.

No surviving source in the current bundle gives an hour-by-hour progression. For **scene blocking only**, order-of-magnitude bands of tens of minutes to a few hours are acceptable: the first keten can become a developed building fire within the opening minutes; neighbouring industrial structures can be involved during roughly the first half-hour; secondary harbour/northern-edge ignitions can plausibly appear within the first hour; separate pockets can merge around inner harbour, watermill/Molenwater and north-western blocks within roughly one to two hours; destructive expansion can plausibly reach the Kruisbroeders/western fabric over the first few hours while individual structures continue to burn much longer. These are explicitly **modelled bands, never archival clock times**.

The resolved family home in the older Nieuwstraat/Armenhoek is **not** declared destroyed: no current parcel-level source proves that. The preferred scene-blocking proposal is therefore smaller and more immediate. Claes first experiences wind, smoke, ash and falling glowing particles; a roof edge, wooden outbuilding or stored material at the household can ignite locally through wind-driven firebrands and still be fought while the main dwelling survives. Cornelis can thus spend decisive time saving Claes and the home even as harbour-linked stores, capital, routes, educational/social infrastructure or people elsewhere become unreachable. The family can win the immediate fight for the house and still lose the larger world around it. This detail remains **PROPOSED scene blocking**, not historical parcel fact.

The human toll of the fire is **UNKNOWN** in the current evidence bundle. The consulted fire-specific sources give no reliable number of dead or injured. No number is not evidence of zero casualties, but a generic statement that early-modern city fires could kill people is not proof that anyone died in this specific Goese fire. Claes' mother's fate is therefore explicitly open under `OPEN.MOTHER.FIRE_1554.001`. If the author later chooses to make her a fatal casualty, her death is fictional. The spatially strongest options are that she is already in the heavily affected harbour/Turfkade/Kruisbroeders corridor, that she re-enters or tries to cross that corridor and is cut off by new spot fires or collapse, or that she is recovered alive with burns, smoke injury or collapse trauma and dies later. Killing her through total destruction of the family home is the weaker construction because that parcel is not historically proven destroyed and the current scene model allows the dwelling to survive. A death choice would also supersede the currently open question of her adult function after Cornelis' later fall; survival keeps `OPEN.MOTHER.ADULT_FUNCTION.001` active.

The **1572 siege** is a second and different destruction layer. The strongest pressure fell at the 's-Heer Hendrikskinderenpoort and the harbour access. Outside salt works were deliberately burned for military reasons and a brewery in the Voorstad was reported burned; Mondragón relieved the city in October. The reported burned Voorstad brewery must not be conflated with the documented Nissepad brewery that appears intact/equipped in 1577. If the novel makes Cornelis' fictional business the 1572 loss, that requires a story decision linking him to the **Voorstad brewery**, not a retrospective claim that the 1577 Nissepad brewery had burned. Post-war sales in 1577–1579 may be used as a pattern of liquidation/reorganisation only where the acts support it; they must not be called executions or confiscations without explicit legal evidence.

The **Goese rederijker meeting place is likewise resolved without flattening the institutional chronology** (`DEC.GOES.REDERIJKERS.MEETINGPLACE.2026-08-14`). The best-documented old chamber, the Nardusbloem, met in the **Zusterhuis** until 1626 and only then moved to the building/hof of the handbow guild Sint-Sebastiaan. The Sint-Sebastiaanshof is therefore a later location and must not be back-projected into Cornelis' mid-sixteenth-century scenes. Cornelis' rederijker meetings are staged in the Zusterhuis/former Zwarte-Zusters complex associated with the Singelstraat. This does not prove that every separately named Goese chamber — Goudtsblome, Clisblome, Nardusbloem and later Edele Castanienbloem — used the same room. The exact named chamber to which fictional Cornelis belongs remains separately open under `OPEN.CORNELIS.REDERIJKERS.CHAMBER.001`. Maria Magdalena devotion, the worldly Zusterhuis meeting environment and the later Sint-Sebastiaanshof are distinct relations.

For a concrete Goes scene the required spatial answer is: **where the character is in that year; what relation binds them to that place; the year-valid route; streets/gates/landmarks passed; named or generic people/trades available from the register layer; event damage/change since the previous time slice; and explicit certainty/open labels.**

### Goes — Grote of Maria Magdalenakerk

The Erfgoed van Goes dossier and associated StoryMap now form a dedicated evidence bundle (`SRC-HIST-GOES-GROTEKERK-001`, `SC.HIST.GOES_GROTE_KERK.*`) and a proposed worldbuilding module (`WORLD.GOES_GREAT_CHURCH`). They materially sharpen the word **church** in Claes' youth architecture: before 1578 this is not the later white, relatively empty Protestant interior, but a dense Catholic spatial and sensory system. The late-medieval choir dates from the 1455 campaign (StoryMap 1455–1475), the transepts from around 1500 (StoryMap 1500–1508), and Claes' period uses the older pre-1618 nave rather than the higher and longer nave rebuilt after the 1618 fire.

Before 1578 the church contained roughly thirty large and small altars. In the central choir stood the high altar, sacrament tower and sacrament altar, Gospel and Epistle lecterns and the pastor's seat; the present choir also contained at least twelve guild chapels or altars. Named nave altars include St Christopher of the beer carriers, St Apollonia of the sack carriers, Three Kings, St Barbara, St Anthony, Sweet Name of Jesus, St Peter, St Andrew, St Agnes and Holy Trinity. Their precise positions are explicitly not all known. The Institute of the Seven Hours, founded in 1440, gives the church a recurring daily psalmic soundscape. In 1550 father and son Niehoff built a new organ at the corner of the choir and north transept, making the instrument available to Claes from about age seven or eight onward.

The northern choir closure was the renewed Onze Lieve Vrouwekapel. The southern closure probably contained a shrine or chapel of Maria Magdalena, patroness of Goes, who is explicitly described as also being venerated by the Goes rhetoricians; the StoryMap associates this chapel with the 1552 triptych for Matthijs van der Straten. This supports cultural and devotional relevance for Cornelis as a rhetorician, but **does not** prove that the Edele Castanienbloem owned the chapel, met there, possessed an altar there or held formal rights in it.

The StoryMap can be used for scene blocking only with its uncertainty labels intact. It reconstructs the four crossing pillars as Gregorius (NW), Ambrosius (SW), Augustinus (SE) and Hieronymus (NE), and assigns apostolic identities to twelve choir pillars; the accompanying wording says the apostle attribution is probable, so the exact individual sequence remains reconstruction rather than archival certainty. Other usable source-backed details include the St Gregory Mass relief in the north transept, pest grooves in the facade, a public punishment bench, old anchorite cells, a baptismal chapel and a c. 1525 ancillary room. Fifteenth-century anchorites are documented; a living anchorite in Claes' youth is not.

The building must be time-sensitive across Claes' life. In 1578 the Catholic liturgical and visual order changes: altars lose their Catholic function, the Seven Hours cease, and saint imagery is whitewashed or removed from view, while some traces are hidden, stored or survive materially. In 1594 a new pulpit is attached to a choir pillar. This creates a powerful but historically grounded continuity motif: **carrier remains, function and readable meaning change**. It may resonate with Claes' wider carrier/content architecture, but that resonance is authorial interpretation rather than a historical claim about the church.

Guardrails: do not equate the beer carriers' guild with brewers; do not infer Cornelis' membership or altar rights from his beer trade; do not use StoryMap pins as exact proof where the source says *vermoedelijk*, *waarschijnlijk* or location unknown; and never back-project the post-1618 nave, later walking-church arrangement or modern interior into Claes' sixteenth-century scenes.

Huis der Liefde/Radermacher material may function as network or late spiritual/social mirror but never as unsupported proof of formal Familist membership or proto-Masonic institutional continuity.
