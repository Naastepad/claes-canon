# Aernouldt de Hont — shipping profile, ca. 1558–1589

**Status:** ACTIVE AUTHORING DOMAIN  
**Historical person:** Aernouldt de Hont, documented 1589 as `schipper van Delft`  
**Novel role:** Cornelis' preferred recurring schipper from the late 1550s / early 1560s through 1569  
**Core boundary:** vessel family and Cornelis relationship are novel reconstruction; historical vessel remains unknown

## 1. Preferred vessel image: the heudeschip

The preferred authoring vessel for De Hont is now an **ordinary regional heudeschip / heude**.

This is stronger than the former generic `vrachtschuit` default because three evidence layers converge:

1. historical ship-type literature places heuden on the South-Holland and Zeeland waters in the sixteenth century;
2. a Goese contract of **8 May 1595** names the heudeschip **Den Draeck** in recurring freight service;
3. a 1627 Rijksmuseum print labels an ordinary heude at about **20 last**, supplying a near-contemporary visual/capacity comparator.

The historical source for De Hont himself still says only `schipper van Delft`. Therefore:

> **In the novel De Hont sails a heudeschip. In history we do not know what vessel he sailed.**

### Physical authoring scale

Use a medium regional vessel:

- authoring band: roughly **15–25 last**;
- default mental image: approximately **20 last**;
- shallow-water/coastal freight rather than prestigious blue-water shipping;
- enough burden for substantial mixed cargo, but small enough for the channels, tides and regional harbours relevant to Goes.

Do not use the documented 40–50-last sea-going heuden that sailed to England as the default.

### Ownership

Leave De Hont's legal ownership **OPEN**.

A 1585 Goese record proves that a `gemeen heudeschip` could be jointly held. A shipmaster can therefore plausibly be owner, part-owner, or master of another person's vessel without the novel needing to settle this immediately.

Preferred current authoring attitude:

> De Hont has enough long-term stake and authority in the vessel to behave as its master, but no exact ownership fraction is stated.

## 2. Cargo scale

### Beer

Historical freight arithmetic can reckon **1 ship last = 18 beer tons / Antwerp amens**. A 15–25-last vessel therefore has a theoretical all-beer capacity of roughly **270–450 casks** in that arithmetic.

That is a **sanity ceiling**, not the expected Nissepat load.

Cornelis' own pre-fire annual beer throughput remains calibrated at roughly **200–350 casks per year**. His beer does not need a dedicated ship. The normal visual model is mixed freight shared with other merchants.

Use:

- **30–80 beer casks** = routine Cornelis consignment;
- **80–150 casks** = deliberately large Cornelis consignment;
- more than this = possible only when the scene deliberately makes the shipment exceptional or near-dedicated.

The separate 1589 Goese record of **52 tons of English beer** being transshipped and carried onward already shows that `dozens of beer tons` is a normal historical order of magnitude in this regional system.

### Beer-cask volume

Do not silently turn `ton` into one fixed litre value.

For visualisation only:

- Antwerp beer vessels in the sixteenth century are around the order of 140–150 litres depending on the exact definition/date;
- later Goese measures produce a different figure;
- Delft itself could use a beer ton differing from Amsterdam.

In prose, prefer **ton**, **vat**, **half vat**, number of casks and physical handling over litres.

### Salt

Salt behaves very differently from beer.

Boonman's synthesis gives roughly **7 last per `honderd` salt** and describes large salt-ship cargoes around 1570 commonly at **10–20 hundred**, sometimes more. Those are far larger cargoes than the ordinary De Hont model.

Therefore Cornelis does **not** send a giant salt-ship load each time.

Scene calibration:

- **0.5–1.5 hundred salt** = plausible mixed-freight parcel;
- around **2 hundred** = substantial / near-dedicated heude cargo;
- bigger = requires a separate vessel/charter/plot reason.

This reinforces the economic picture of Cornelis: his Antwerp salt trade can be regular and valuable without making him the owner of a large salt fleet.

## 3. Routes

### Goes ↔ Antwerp

This route is not an invented clandestine corridor. A sixteenth-century KZGW reconstruction states that **Goes had a beurtveer on Antwerp**; Biezelinge also maintained Antwerp traffic, and both the older Scheldt/Oosterscheldt route and the increasingly important Honte/Westerscheldt route formed part of South-Beveland's commercial geography.

So Cornelis' relationship with Antwerp can be wholly legitimate before it ever carries sensitive paper.

Preferred authoring route logic:

`Goes harbour -> tidal regional waters -> Scheldt/Honte system -> Antwerp`

The exact channel used can vary by year, tide and navigation and should not be hard-coded as one invariant track.

### Goes ↔ Delft

Delft beer reaches Zeeland through ordinary waterborne commerce. De Hont's later historical label `schipper van Delft` makes this direction especially natural.

Levendale's own 1590 travel notes give a useful comparator: after boarding ship he reached Vlaardingen the following day and went on to Delft; on return he boarded at Delfshaven on 7 September, passed through the Spui on 8 September and reached Goes on 9 September.

This is not a freight timetable, but it makes a multi-day Delft connection concrete.

## 4. How long does a voyage take?

### Authoring range — not timetable

**Goes–Antwerp**

- favourable non-war movement: roughly **1–2 travel days**;
- for planning a scene, allow **2–3 days** when tide, loading, waiting, inspection or poor wind matter;
- in wartime it can take substantially longer or require staged military-controlled travel.

Levendale's October 1589 journey demonstrates the wartime version: Goes to Waarde by wagon on 9 October, late-night embarkation, Fort Lillo on the 10th, Antwerp on the 11th.

**Goes–Delft**

- default planning: roughly **2 calendar days**;
- good conditions can compress the movement;
- three days or more is entirely usable when wind, tide, transfer or waiting intervene.

### Prose rule

Write:

- `met het tij`;
- `bij de volgende vloed`;
- `als de wind bleef staan`;
- `een dag later dan beloofd`;
- `hij lag nog te wachten op water`.

Avoid fake precision such as `achttien uur naar Antwerpen`.

## 5. What does a freight agreement look like?

The best direct local comparator is the Goese 1595 agreement for **Den Draeck**:

- named carrier/master;
- named vessel;
- defined service;
- **two-year duration**;
- normally **one voyage per week**;
- explicit dependency on `weder, windt ende open waeter`;
- fixed payment **per voyage**;
- part payment/benefit in kind.

This means a Cornelis–De Hont relationship does not need to be reinvented as a modern bill of lading for every trip.

Preferred model:

### Standing relationship

Cornelis and De Hont know each other's expectations and settle recurring business repeatedly.

### Cargo-specific settlement

Individual movements can still specify:

- number/kind of casks or salt units;
- destination/receiver;
- freight payment;
- who arranges loading and unloading;
- duties/handling/pilotage if applicable;
- cask return;
- payment timing;
- condition/shortage on delivery;
- special war or weather risk.

## 6. Risk split

Do not use a modern `carrier is liable for everything` model.

For authoring, distinguish four risk baskets.

### A. De Hont / vessel side

Default responsibility:

- seaworthy/usable vessel for the agreed voyage;
- crew and ordinary navigation;
- avoidable loss caused by bad handling or preventable negligence;
- executing the agreed movement once the voyage can reasonably proceed.

The sixteenth-century Southern-Low-Countries legal trend moves toward stricter shipmaster liability for preventable loss.

### B. Cornelis / merchant side

Default responsibility:

- market price after arrival;
- customer's ability or refusal to pay;
- inherent quality problems in cargo not caused by transport;
- commercial choice to hold or release stock;
- his own packing/cask problem if shown to predate handover.

### C. Ordinary navigation uncertainty

Weather, wind, tide and waiting are not automatically fault. The 1595 Goese contract itself conditions performance on weather, wind and open water.

### D. Extraordinary common peril

If goods are deliberately sacrificed or extraordinary expense is incurred to save ship and cargo, sixteenth-century Southern-Low-Countries practice can move toward **general-average style sharing** among the interests in the voyage.

Do not force this machinery into every local mishap. It matters when the scene has real common peril.

## 7. Beer leakage, souring and broken casks

Keep this evidentially honest.

The exact Cornelis–De Hont rule is fictional and the conditions of the 1589 Horen–De Hont agreement have not been recovered in the consulted extract.

Therefore a scene may legitimately contain a dispute such as:

- `the cask was already bad when loaded`;
- `the hoop loosened under your hands`;
- `you left us waiting through two tides`;
- `the beer soured before it reached the tapper`;
- `three casks arrived short`.

But the narrator should not settle the law before the characters do. Measurement, witnesses, accounts and reputation are the historically richer mechanisms.

## 8. Why De Hont matters to Cornelis

De Hont is not important because Cornelis owns a ship through him.

He matters because he reduces **transaction friction**:

- Cornelis does not renegotiate trust from zero for every cargo;
- De Hont knows the Goese harbour and receivers;
- he knows which contacts in Delft/Holland can be trusted;
- he knows where Cornelis' salt or beer normally goes;
- he can carry ordinary business messages with the freight;
- he creates continuity when Cornelis' own capital weakens after 1554.

After the fire, this matters more, not less. Cornelis has lost productive capital and liquidity. A reliable shipper allows him to keep trading with **network capital**.

## 9. Clandestine paper guardrail

The ordinary legal route is what makes later sensitive transport plausible.

Never begin with:

`De Hont is a smuggler.`

Begin with:

`De Hont is the man who already carries ordinary cargo and knows where it must go.`

If paper/books enter a scene, choose explicitly whether he:

1. knows exactly what he carries;
2. suspects but does not ask;
3. knows only the declared commercial consignment.

That is a plot decision per scene. No historical evidence currently makes him a clandestine carrier.

## 10. Hard boundaries

Never present as historical fact:

- that De Hont owned a heudeschip;
- that his vessel was exactly 20 last;
- that he carried 30–80 Nissepat casks;
- that he carried one hundred Nissepat salt;
- that Goes–Antwerp always took one or two days;
- that Cornelis paid a particular freight rate;
- that De Hont bore a fixed historical leakage percentage;
- that the 1589 Horen agreement used these fictional terms.

These are **authoring calibrations built on a historically constrained transport system**.
