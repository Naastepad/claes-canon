# Aernouldt de Hont — shipping context, ca. 1558–1589

**Status:** ACTIVE AUTHORING DOMAIN  
**Historical anchor:** 2 May 1589, Aernouldt de Hont, `schipper van Delft`, contracts with Goese biersteker Michiel Michielsz Horen concerning beers De Hont will bring to Goes (RAZE 2039, fol. 86, via Levendale transcription).  
**Fiction boundary:** De Hont's earlier relationship with Cornelis is novel canon; his exact vessel, capacity and pre-1589 voyages are not historically recovered.

## 1. What kind of ship?

The 1589 act calls De Hont only **schipper van Delft**. It does not name his vessel.

### Canon rule

Do **not** give De Hont a historically asserted ship type unless a new source identifies it.

### Authoring default

Treat his working vessel as a **small-to-medium decked freight craft suitable for inland, tidal and estuarine waters, with relatively shallow draught**.

A `pleit` is a useful visual/comparative model because pleiten are documented as characteristic freight carriers on Zeeland/Flemish/Brabant waterways, but **`De Hont owned a pleit` is not canon**.

Likewise, later Zeeland material gives `smalschip`, `kromstevenschuit` and `crabschuit` as useful size/type comparators, but none identifies De Hont's vessel.

### Why shallow draught matters

Mid-sixteenth-century routes between Holland/Zeeland and Antwerp crossed rapidly changing tidal waters. The eastern Scheldt/Kreekrak passage remained navigable but increasingly shallow and tide-dependent, while the Honte/Westerschelde became the principal deep-water route to Antwerp.

A shallow-draught freight craft therefore gives De Hont a believable commercial advantage without turning him into an exceptional ocean-going captain.

## 2. Capacity — authoring calibration, not a recovered measurement

For scene planning use the following **calibration band** only:

- useful payload: roughly **20–40 metric tonnes equivalent**;
- a beer-heavy voyage: roughly **80–150 full casks** as a practical story range, depending on actual cask size, stowage and mixed cargo;
- mixed cargo: fewer beer casks plus salt, grain, return empties, letters or other merchants' goods;
- salt-heavy cargo: think in **tens of tonnes**, not a few household sacks.

These figures are not a historical measurement of De Hont's ship. They are a conservative authoring envelope based on near-period small/medium freight-craft comparators and the physical mass/volume of casked beer.

### Guardrail

Do not turn this calibration into a statement such as `De Hont's ship was 30 tons` or `carried exactly 120 casks` unless a scene requires a fictional load and labels it as such internally.

## 3. Delft beer logistics

Delft was one of the largest sixteenth-century beer-export centres. Contemporary/near-contemporary descriptions explicitly state that Delft beer was shipped in large quantities to Zeeland and elsewhere.

Therefore the 1589 Horen–De Hont contract is economically ordinary, not exceptional:

`Delft brewery / supplier -> water transport -> De Hont -> Goes -> Horen as biersteker -> tappers / local consumers`

For Cornelis' earlier fictional relationship the same infrastructure can work in both directions:

- De Hont can bring Delft/Holland beer or other goods toward Goes;
- Cornelis can use him for selected outward consignments and for onward coordination;
- Cornelis does not need to own a ship to have a stable maritime trade network.

## 4. Goes -> Antwerp

### Historical geography

By the middle of the sixteenth century the **Honte/Westerschelde** had become the major deep-water approach to Antwerp. The older eastern route through Eendracht/Oosterschelde/Kreekrak remained usable for suitable vessels but was increasingly shallow and highly tide-dependent.

### Authoring travel time

Use:

- **favourable wind + tide:** a long single sailing day is possible for a small/shallow vessel;
- **normal planning assumption:** **1–2 days** from Goes to Antwerp;
- **bad wind, tide, fog, grounding risk or waiting:** **3+ days** is entirely believable.

This is **authoring inference**, not a recovered sixteenth-century timetable.

The relevant dramatic variable is not a fixed modern ETA but a departure window governed by **tide + wind + depth + daylight + local pilotage/knowledge**.

## 5. Goes <-> Delft

Delft had maritime access through Delfshaven and a well-developed beer-export network.

For scene planning use:

- **normal cargo journey:** about **2–3 days** between the Goes system and Delft/Delfshaven;
- **with poor conditions, waiting or handling delays:** **3–5 days or more**;
- loading, cask handling and waiting for a favourable departure can add as much narrative time as the sailing itself.

Again this is authoring calibration, not a historical schedule for De Hont.

## 6. Who pays and who bears the risk?

Do not model the relationship as a modern parcel carrier.

### Cornelis / cargo interest normally carries

- purchase price and working capital;
- market-price risk;
- customer default / credit risk;
- inherent commodity risk not caused by the carrier;
- war/blockade/route-commercial consequences unless separately shifted by agreement;
- the economic loss if a legitimate shipment cannot be sold at the expected price.

### De Hont / master can be responsible for

- negligent loading or stowage;
- bad securing of casks;
- defective tackle/handling attributable to ship and crew;
- imprudent departure/navigation where fault can be established;
- failure to follow the agreed commercial voyage without justification.

### Common peril

Early-modern maritime custom recognised shared-loss logic for extraordinary sacrifices made to save ship and cargo, such as jettison or cutting equipment in extremis.

So a storm scene should distinguish:

`bad luck / peril of the water` from `De Hont's negligence` from `general-average type sacrifice`.

## 7. Freight contract model

The actual 1589 transcription states only that Horen will be biersteker of the beers De Hont brings to Goes **according to conditions**; the surviving extract does not reproduce those conditions.

For Cornelis scenes therefore use a flexible model:

- freight can be agreed per cask, parcel, voyage or mixed consignment;
- De Hont may carry other merchants' goods at the same time;
- payment may be partly dependent on delivery/account settlement;
- return empties can be a real logistical obligation;
- do not invent a standard freight rate until a local tariff/contract is found.

## 8. De Hont's status

Treat De Hont as an **independent maritime tradesman/entrepreneur**, not merely Cornelis' wage servant.

Historically, regional schippers often owned a vessel or share in one and contracted freight for others; exact ownership in De Hont's case remains OPEN.

That gives the relationship the right social texture:

**Cornelis and De Hont are recurring commercial partners with different capital and expertise, not master and employee.**

De Hont's capital is vessel + seamanship + route knowledge + contacts. Cornelis' capital is goods + credit + customers + salt/bier trade + commercial information.

## 9. Why Cornelis values this particular schipper

The relationship becomes valuable because De Hont can repeatedly provide:

- predictable handling of valuable casks and salt cargo;
- knowledge of shallows and tide windows;
- trustworthy account of shortages/leakage on arrival;
- familiarity with Goese receivers and Delft/Holland suppliers;
- continuity when Cornelis is not personally travelling;
- a legitimate carrier network that can later, scene by scene, also move sensitive papers without making De Hont automatically a smuggler.

## Never write as recovered fact

- De Hont's ship type, ship name, dimensions or tonnage;
- that he owned 100% of the vessel;
- a fixed one-day Goes–Antwerp timetable;
- an exact Delft–Goes timetable;
- that every loss at sea was Cornelis' risk;
- that every damaged cask was De Hont's liability;
- that De Hont regularly used the Kreekrak rather than the Honte, or vice versa;
- a fixed freight price per cask;
- an exact number of casks on his historical 1589 voyage.
