# De Hont route, vessel and contract refinement, 1551–1589

**Status:** ACTIVE AUTHORING REFINEMENT  
**Parent domain:** `CORNELIS_DE_HONT_HOREN_TRADE_CONTINUITY_1550_1589.md`  
**Purpose:** tighten route/vessel/contract mechanics without inventing the missing terms of RAZE 2039 fol. 86

## Executive decision

The new evidence makes the **corridors** firmer but does **not** justify fixing Aernouldt de Hont's exact vessel.

Use this hierarchy:

1. **De Hont in prose:** `schipper`, `schuit`, `vrachtschuit`, or a concrete physical description without a type-name.
2. **Delft beer logistics:** think in beer schuiten / heavily laden cargo schuiten moving through the Schie-Delfshaven system.
3. **Antwerp type candidate:** a `kagheschuyte` is now a strong researched fictional option because one from Delft is documented reaching Antwerp with cargo in 1585.
4. **Kromsteven / krabschuit:** retain as researched regional analogues, not as the default identity of De Hont's vessel.

Do not turn any of these candidates into historical De Hont biography unless a De Hont-specific vessel record is recovered.

# 1. Delft / Delfshaven -> Goes is now direct period evidence

The strongest new anchor is a Delft municipal account for **1572**.

It records vessels lying both before Delft and at Delfshaven that were requisitioned to carry Barthel Entes and troops from Holland **to Goes**. Twenty-one vessels were later compensated after also being held before Goes.

Source claim:

- `SC.HIST.DELFT_DELFSHAVEN.GOES.VESSEL_CORRIDOR.1572.001`

### Consequence

The route family

`Delft / Delfshaven -> Goes`

is no longer supported only by near-contemporary 1608-1609 analogues. It is physically attested in Claes' own period.

### Guardrail

The 1572 account is military transport, not an ordinary freight contract. Its 42-guilder allowance per one group of vessels is **not** De Hont's freight rate and must never be converted into one.

The entry also does not tell us:

- which exact channel each vessel used;
- vessel types;
- capacity;
- normal commercial duration;
- whether goods from Delft city remained on the same hull all the way to Goes.

# 2. Delft's beer logistics before open water

The Delft system itself now has a better material model.

A historical synthesis reports a **1548** memorandum by Delft pensionary Willem Criep describing beer schuiten being pulled along the Schie toward the Maas. In **1551**, Hague beer carriers obtained permission to use horses because their **heavy schuiten carrying Delft beer** were burdensome to pull by human force.

Source claims:

- `SC.HIST.DELFT.BEERSCHUIT.SCHIE_MAAS.1548.001`
- `SC.HIST.DELFT.HEAVY_BEER_SCHUIT.HORSE_TOWING.1551.001`

### Scene model

For a Delft-origin beer movement, think in logistical legs rather than one modern point-to-point trip:

`brewery / urban quay -> Delft beer schuit -> Schie -> Delfshaven / Maas-side interface -> onward regional water transport -> Zeeland / Goes`

This is a **routing model**, not proof of mandatory transshipment. Whether De Hont stays with one vessel, receives cargo at Delfshaven, or participates in a transfer remains OPEN.

That uncertainty is useful: `schipper van Delft` need not mean every cargo was physically loaded beside a Delft brewery.

# 3. Delft -> Antwerp: a much stronger vessel/corridor analogue

A contemporary merchant letter cited by Jongbloet-van Houtte records that on **19 February 1585** a **`kagheschuyte` from Delft**, loaded with butter and cheese, reached Antwerp through Parma's blockade.

Source claim:

- `SC.HIST.DELFT.ANTWERP.KAGHESCHUYTE.1585.001`

This is much stronger for De Hont's possible Antwerp traffic than a generic statement that Hollanders sailed there. It proves that a Delft-associated `kagheschuyte` could make the Delft/Antwerp commercial corridor in the late sixteenth century.

### What it permits

If an Antwerp scene needs a researched fictional vessel type for De Hont, **kagheschuyte is now a defensible first candidate**.

### What it does not permit

Do not write:

- `De Hont owned a kagheschuyte` as recovered fact;
- that his 1589 Goes vessel was the same type;
- that butter/cheese carriage proves beer or salt capacity;
- that blockade-running describes his normal route or politics.

# 4. Broader Holland -> Antwerp traffic is not exceptional

Asaert's synthesis of Antwerp shipping shows substantial Holland involvement in the mid-sixteenth-century port. For 1555-1558, the cited research gives Holland the largest share of non-Antwerp oversea traffic in the sample and shows overwhelmingly northern participation in the sampled grain deliveries.

Source claim:

- `SC.HIST.HOLLAND.ANTWERP.SHIPPING_WEIGHT.1555_1558.001`

This means a Delft/Holland skipper operating into Antwerp in Cornelis' commercial world requires no exotic explanation.

It still does **not** prove De Hont himself sailed there before 1569. That remains explicit novel backprojection.

# 5. Vessel size and beer capacity: still do not invent a number

The evidence now gives us three different scales which must not be collapsed:

- a **3-last kromstevenschuit** in Holland in 1582;
- **heavy Delft beer schuiten** as a functional transport description in 1551;
- a **52-ton beer consignment** transshipped at Goes in 1589.

None tells us De Hont's carrying capacity.

Therefore:

- no canonical number of beer tons for De Hont;
- no conversion of vessel `last` into beer `ton` without a metrological bridge;
- no assumption that the 52-ton consignment fitted one vessel;
- no inference from `heavy schuit` to measured tonnage.

For scenes, `several / many casks`, `a hold or deck worked around casks`, or another non-numeric material description is safer than a fabricated capacity.

# 6. What a beer-distribution contract could actually care about

We still do not have the missing conditions after `volgen voorwaarden` in the Horen-De Hont 1589 extract.

However, an Antwerp beer-distribution contract of **1446** gives a concrete historical mechanism comparator. It linked a Gouda skipper/supplier side to a local Antwerp distributor and explicitly dealt with:

- compensation per vat sold;
- collection of empty casks from customers;
- return of empties to the waterfront;
- working capital supplied to the local distributor;
- repayment on termination;
- compensation for missing casks.

Source claim:

- `SC.HIST.ANTWERP.BEER_DISTRIBUTION.CASK_RETURN_CREDIT.1446.001`

### Why this matters

This makes several things scene-real rather than generic modern business invention:

`full cask -> local sale -> credit / commission -> empty cask -> collection -> return -> missing-cask liability`

The cask is not disposable packaging. It can carry capital, obligation and memory back through the network.

### Hard boundary

The 1446 contract is more than a century earlier and from Gouda-Antwerp. Its exact rates and conditions are **not** imported into Cornelis-De Hont or Horen-De Hont.

Use it to define the **kind of questions a contract may answer**, not the answers.

# 7. Financial risk: what is safe now

## Historically safe pressures

A Cornelis-De Hont scene may legitimately turn on:

- freight and handling;
- whether cargo is accepted at delivery;
- leakage / breakage / souring;
- delay by water, wind, conflict or loading;
- credit to local buyers;
- return of empty casks;
- missing casks;
- money tied up while beer or packaging is elsewhere;
- proof of delivery / accounts / witnesses;
- transshipment risk.

These mechanisms are supported across the existing beer/shipping evidence.

## Still OPEN

For the actual 1589 Horen-De Hont agreement we do not yet know:

- owner of the beer before arrival;
- freight payer;
- whether Horen bought outright or sold on commission;
- exclusivity;
- exact commission;
- risk transfer point;
- leakage allowance;
- cask ownership;
- cask-return terms;
- payment timing.

Do not resolve these by importing general maritime law.

## Fiction-ready but not yet canonized option

If a future Cornelis scene needs a bespoke arrangement, the historically congenial architecture is:

`merchant/supplier capital + trusted carrier + delivery/accounting moment + reusable casks + local distributor/credit + return obligations`

The exact division of loss between Cornelis and De Hont remains an **authorial decision**, not a research conclusion.

# 8. Duration: research result is still OPEN, and that is the correct result

The new sources prove routes and traffic but do not supply a trustworthy sixteenth-century Delft-Goes or Goes-Antwerp transit time.

Therefore retain the existing rule:

> **write in tides and days, not clock-hours.**

For authoring, split the journey mentally into:

1. urban/canal handling;
2. Delft-Schie/Delfshaven movement where relevant;
3. open/tidal regional leg;
4. Zeeland/Scheldt approach;
5. waiting, unloading and possible transshipment.

A missed tide, contrary wind, crowded quay, inspection or wartime interruption can move arrival without requiring exposition.

Do not canonize `Delft -> Goes = X hours` or `Goes -> Antwerp = Y day` until route-specific contemporary evidence is recovered.

# 9. Best-fit portrayal of Aernouldt de Hont

The best current portrayal is **not** `captain of a named type of ship`.

It is:

> a Delft-associated regional cargo skipper whose value lies in knowing how to get goods through a chain of waterways, tides, quays, receivers, casks, debts and returns.

That is materially stronger than giving him a prestigious ship name or invented tonnage.

For Claes, De Hont can be recognizable through practice:

- how he judges whether a cask has leaked;
- where he wants a mark placed;
- which empties must come back;
- who signs or witnesses receipt;
- when he refuses to leave on the wrong water;
- whom he trusts at a quay;
- which debt or message travels with the cargo.

This allows the later Horen-De Hont agreement of 1589 to feel like the survival of a commercial grammar Claes knew from Cornelis, while the historical/fictive boundary remains intact.
