# De Hont shipping, route and freight-contract authoring guide, ca. 1558–1589

**Status:** ACTIVE AUTHORING SUPPLEMENT  
**Parent domain:** `CORNELIS_DE_HONT_HOREN_TRADE_CONTINUITY_1550_1589.md`  
**Purpose:** make De Hont usable in scenes without converting analogues into false biography  
**Core rule:** historical mechanism may be specific; De Hont-specific biography stays only as specific as the evidence

## 1. Executive authoring answer

For prose, Aernouldt de Hont should normally sail a **schuit / vrachtschuit**: a practical shallow-water regional cargo craft, not a prestigious seagoing ship.

If a scene genuinely needs a researched visual subtype, **kromstevenschuit** is the strongest current fictional working analogue because it is documented:

- in the Holland/Delft-adjacent world at Overschie in 1582;
- with a Reimerswaal connection in 1594;
- and, just outside the story period, in a Delfshaven–Goes transaction in 1608.

A **krabschuit** is an equally period-real alternative with especially strong Scheldt/Zeeland value: one is documented in ordinary cargo work on the Scheldt near Goes in 1586, and the type also appears in a later Delfshaven–Goes context.

**Do not promote either name to historical fact about De Hont.** If type is not plot-relevant, keep the manuscript at `schuit`.

## 2. What can we say about capacity?

### De Hont-specific capacity: OPEN

No source recovered so far gives his vessel type, `last` rating, barrel count or carrying capacity.

### What the surrounding evidence does give

- A 1582 **kromstevenschuit** is described as `groot 3 last`. This proves a genuinely small commercial craft could be expressed at that scale, but vessel `last` cannot be converted into beer tons by importing a commodity definition from another source.
- A separate **Goese 1589** transaction involved **52 tons of English beer** being taken over at Goes for onward freight to Zierikzee. This proves that a regional beer movement in the **tens of casks/tons** was operationally real.
- The 52-ton movement does not identify the vessel type and is not De Hont's capacity.
- A Holland beer ton around 1590 can be visualised with a scholarly proxy of roughly 154 litres, but Delft used a different local ton measure. Keep `tonnen bier` in historical prose.

### Scene-safe scale rule

Use three layers rather than manufacturing a vessel capacity:

1. **individual sale / delivery:** one or a few casks can move through the distribution system;
2. **ordinary visible cargo:** several or many casks can legitimately fill a loading/unloading scene;
3. **large regional consignment:** `tientallen tonnen bier` is directly supported as a 1589 Goese shipment scale.

If the plot ever requires an exact number of casks aboard **De Hont's own ship**, that number is an explicit fiction design parameter and must be recorded as such. Do not derive it from `3 last` or `52 tons`.

## 3. Delft / Delfshaven -> Goes: corridor evidence

The route is no longer merely inferred from generic geography.

Delft municipal accounts for **1572** record vessels lying before Delft and at **Delfshaven** being requisitioned to carry Barthel Entes and troops from Holland toward **Goes**. Twenty-one vessels subsequently received an allowance in the accounts.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.DELFT.GOES.SHIPPING_CORRIDOR.1572.001`.

This does not prove De Hont sailed in 1572 and it is not civilian beer freight. It does prove that the Delft/Delfshaven–Goes corridor functioned operationally in the correct century and geography.

Combined with Delft-beer distribution evidence, De Hont's fictional Holland–Zeeland commercial role is therefore a **bounded historical backprojection**, not a route invented from a modern map.

## 4. Goes -> Antwerp: first direct route-time comparator

### Levendale, October 1589

Pieter Cornelisz van Levendale records the following outbound sequence:

- **9 October:** by wagon from Goes to **Waarde**;
- late evening: embarkation in a `roeijschute van oorloghe` in the Geuzen fleet;
- **10 October:** at **Fort Lillo**;
- **11 October:** onward past fortified positions and into **Antwerp**.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.GOES.ANTWERP.TRAVEL_STAGE.1589.001`.

This is the first direct comparator in the current research layer that gives actual calendar staging from Goes toward Antwerp.

### What it means

It supports a **multi-day route scale** and makes three things concrete:

- land and water legs could be combined;
- Lillo / lower-Scheldt control points could become real stages in wartime;
- transfer, tide and political control matter as much as nominal distance.

### What it does not mean

Levendale travelled in wartime using a war rowing craft. Therefore:

- `Goes -> Antwerp = exactly two days` is **not** a canon rule;
- De Hont's merchant craft did not necessarily follow the same staging;
- the 1589 military frontier cannot be copied unchanged into Cornelis' 1550s/1560s world.

### Timeline planning rule

For manuscript continuity, reserve movement in **days and tidal stages**, not hours. When no scene requires a more exact duration, a roughly **two-day planning allowance** for a Goes–Antwerp movement is a defensible authorial buffer because a direct 1589 comparator fits that order of magnitude. It remains a planning convention, not a recovered universal transit time.

For wartime scenes, allow additional waiting, search, convoy, transfer or political delay. For pre-Revolt scenes, do not import the Lillo/Geuzen frontier apparatus anachronistically.

## 5. The Horen–De Hont act has a physical social setting

The 2 May 1589 agreement was made in **herberg `die Meerminne` in Goes**.

Witnesses:

- **Schobbelandt Pieters**, innkeeper there;
- **Marinus Anthonisz Vocxen**, `panneboeter`;
- both are described as **born in Reimerswaal** and then living in Goes.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.GOES.HOREN_DE_HONT.CONTRACT_SETTING.1589.001`.

This is unusually useful story texture. The archival endpoint of Cornelis' fictional commercial afterlife is literally witnessed by men displaced or migrated from **Reimerswaal into Goes**. That connection may be used as historical atmosphere without making either witness part of Claes' personal circle.

The source still does not reproduce the `voorwaarden` of the agreement.

## 6. Freight contract: historical framework versus novel contract

### Historical mechanism

Sixteenth-century Southern-Low-Countries maritime law and practice distinguish categories that modern prose should not collapse:

- **freight**: the agreed payment for carriage;
- **ordinary voyage costs**: items such as normal pilotage and port dues, which could be allocated by contract;
- **particular loss**: a loss borne by the individually interested party;
- **extraordinary common-safety sacrifice/cost**: the domain of general average;
- **preventable carrier/master fault**: increasingly a basis for shipmaster liability;
- **genuine peril**: a context in which the master retained operational discretion.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.SOUTHERN_LOW_COUNTRIES.FREIGHT_RISK_ALLOCATION.1551_1563.001`.

This is an Antwerp/Southern-Low-Countries framework, not the missing text of fol. 86 and not automatically the custom of every short Zeeland voyage.

## 7. Working Cornelis–De Hont contract model

**Classification: NOVEL AUTHORING MODEL, historically constrained — not a recovered contract.**

When a scene needs the commercial logic between Cornelis and De Hont, use this order:

### Before departure

- cargo is identified by commodity, quantity and packaging (`tonnen`, casks, sacks or other appropriate units);
- freight/payment for carriage is agreed separately from the value of the goods;
- the parties can state who bears ordinary dues, pilotage or loading-related charges;
- destination/receiver is named or made operationally clear;
- unusual instructions belong in the transaction, not in De Hont's permanent job description.

### During carriage

- De Hont controls navigation and immediate vessel safety;
- negligent handling or an avoidable loss is conceptually different from storm, grounding or a sacrifice made for common safety;
- ordinary leakage, broken hoops, delay, wetting or damaged packaging may become a factual dispute, but no fixed historical liability clause may be asserted without scene-specific authorial canon.

### At arrival

- quantity/condition can be checked before commercial hand-off;
- freight settlement and cargo sale need not be the same transaction;
- empty-cask return, shortage and reusable packaging can remain separate obligations;
- the local biersteker/distributor handles the local sales network; customer bad debt is not automatically the shipper's risk.

### Ownership rule

Do **not** make one universal rule that Cornelis always owns the cargo while afloat. Depending on the scene he may be:

- owner/buyer of the goods;
- distributor working on another supplier's account;
- creditor;
- consignee/receiver;
- or commercial intermediary.

The same is true for the 1589 Horen–De Hont act: the surviving extract does not establish who owned the incoming beer.

## 8. Financial risk in scene terms

Use the following distinctions:

| Problem | Default authoring question | Do not assume |
|---|---|---|
| Freight payment | What did Cornelis promise De Hont for carriage? | that freight equals cargo value or commission |
| Port/pilotage cost | Who agreed to pay ordinary voyage charges? | one universal local rule |
| Leakage/broken cask | Was this ordinary loss, poor packing, or negligent handling? | automatic De Hont liability |
| Storm/grounding | Was loss unavoidable or incurred for common safety? | modern insurance payout |
| Cargo sale | Who owned or financed the beer at that point? | that the shipper owned it |
| Customer fails to pay | Whose sale/credit relationship was it? | that maritime freight absorbed bad-debt risk |
| Empty casks | Who must recover/return reusable packaging? | that casks were disposable |

This makes a contract dispute dramatically useful without falsely quoting a law that has not been recovered for the transaction.

## 9. Material authoring texture

Prefer concrete commercial actions over exposition:

- waiting for enough water or a favourable stage of tide;
- counting casks at transfer rather than discussing abstract tonnage;
- checking staves, hoops, leakage and smell;
- distinguishing freight money from money still owed for the beer;
- handing over letters/accounts with the cargo;
- a receiver disputing condition or number;
- delay measured as a missed tide or day rather than `three hours late`;
- a reputable schipper being valuable because he knows receivers, landing practice and who will honour an account.

Do not make De Hont a generic smuggler. His narrative power is that the infrastructure is **legitimate enough to survive its users**.
