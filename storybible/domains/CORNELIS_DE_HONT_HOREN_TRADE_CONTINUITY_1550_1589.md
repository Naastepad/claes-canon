# Cornelis — De Hont — Horen trade continuity, ca. 1550–1589

**Status:** ACTIVE AUTHORING DOMAIN  
**Core:** legitimate commercial continuity across Cornelis' death  
**Historical anchors:** Aernouldt de Hont and Michiel Michielsz Horen, 1588–1589 Goese records  
**Shipping rule:** exact De Hont vessel OPEN; use evidence-bounded schuit analogues rather than invented precision

## Governing idea

The strongest version of Cornelis' shipping network is not an invented anonymous carrier who disappears when Cornelis dies. The novel uses the historically documented Delft schipper **Aernouldt de Hont** as the continuing human link.

### Correct reading of the 1589 agreement

On **2 May 1589**, Goese poorter **Michiel Michielsz Horen** and **Aernouldt de Hont, schipper van Delft**, made a `contract ende accoord van een biersteecker`.

The important direction of the agreement is:

`Aernouldt brings beer to Goes -> Michiel Horen is biersteker for that beer`

The consulted extract says that Horen would be biersteker for such beers as De Hont would bring to Goes, **subject to conditions that the extract does not reproduce**.

That is not the same as saying:

`Horen owns beer -> Horen hires De Hont as carrier`

The latter is **not established** by the source.

Primary locator: `RAZE 2039, fol. 86, 2-5-1589`.

Relevant source claim: `SC.HIST.GOES.HOREN_DE_HONT.BEER_CONTRACT.1589.001`.

## Historical topology versus novel continuity

The historical endpoint gives a clean division of function:

`incoming beer / supplying traffic -> De Hont brings it to Goes -> Horen local biersteker/distribution layer`

The novel deliberately back-projects De Hont into Cornelis' earlier commercial life:

`Cornelis -> trusted schipper De Hont -> Cornelis dies 1569 -> trade fragments / war intervenes -> later Horen consolidates part of the local beer-distribution function -> 1589 real Horen-De Hont agreement`

The 1589 act is therefore a **historical endpoint for a fictional continuity**, not proof that the historical men had this earlier relationship.

## Aernouldt de Hont in Cornelis' period

De Hont is Cornelis' preferred recurring schipper, especially useful for:

- beer and cask traffic involving Delft / Holland and Goes;
- mixed commercial consignments;
- selected salt/export carriage or coordination toward Antwerp;
- payment messages, commercial letters and returns that naturally accompany freight;
- knowledge of tides, routes, receivers, wharf practice and trustworthy intermediaries.

Cornelis does not need to own a vessel. His advantage is that he knows a man who can move goods reliably.

### Evidence boundary

Only De Hont's 1589 status as `schipper van Delft` and his role in the Horen beer-distribution agreement are currently documented. The following remain story extensions or OPEN:

- De Hont working for Cornelis before 1569;
- De Hont carrying Cornelis' salt;
- De Hont sailing to Antwerp for Cornelis;
- the start date of their relationship;
- his age or birth year;
- exact vessel type;
- vessel ownership;
- fixed sailing schedule;
- freight rate;
- beer ownership before arrival;
- cargo-risk allocation.

Do not invent a birth year merely to make the backprojection work.

# What kind of ship should De Hont use?

## Evidence ladder

### A — De Hont-specific evidence

**OPEN.** The 1589 source calls him `schipper van Delft` but gives no vessel name, type, rig, tonnage or ownership.

Therefore no exact vessel type should be promoted to historical fact.

### B — direct period/regional analogues

Several vessel families are demonstrably present in the commercial world around him.

#### Kromstevenschuit — Holland, 1582

A Schiedam archival regest of **14 July 1582** records an Overschie ship carpenter selling a newly launched **kromstevenschuit, groot 3 last**. This establishes the type and a small-vessel capacity expression inside the correct period.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.HOLLAND.KROMSTEVEN.3_LAST.1582.001`.

Important: vessel `last` is not automatically the same thing as a beer-trade `last`; do not convert the three-last figure into beer barrels without a source-specific bridge.

#### Krabschuit — Scheldt near Goes, 1586

A Goese act from **6 March 1586** describes a Gorinchem skipper on the Scheldt near Catshoeck in a `schip ofte crabschuijte` carrying willow planting material. A squall overturned the vessel and salvage had to be organized.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.ZEELAND.CRABSCHUIT.SCHELDT_CARGO_RISK.1586.001`.

This is especially useful because it proves a krabschuit doing ordinary cargo work in the actual Zeeland/Scheldt environment only three years before the De Hont agreement.

#### Kromsteven — Reimerswaal connection, 1594

A Schiedam regest from **1 February 1594** records Joost Engelsz Mol, son of the bailiff of Remmerswaal, paying the final instalment on a newly built kromstevenschuit; a paired entry concerns ironwork for that vessel.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.REIMERSWAAL.KROMSTEVEN.CONNECTION.1594.001`.

This makes the type especially congenial to Claes' geography without proving De Hont used one.

### C — near-contemporary Goes–Delfshaven analogues

The 1608–1609 Wisse register, just outside Claes' 1602 endpoint, gives both:

- a **Delfshaven schipper** selling a `cromsteven schuytte` to a Goese schipper in 1608;
- a **Delfshaven schipper present in Goes** buying a `crabbeschuyt` in 1609.

These are mechanism comparators, not Claes-period events.

Source claims:

- `SC.NEAR.GOES.DELFSHAVEN.KROMSTEVEN_SALE.1608.001`
- `SC.NEAR.GOES.DELFSHAVEN.CRABBSCHUIT.1609.001`

## Authoring decision for scenes

When a scene needs a vessel image but the exact De Hont vessel is not plot-critical:

- write **schuit**, **vrachtschuit**, or another generic period-appropriate description first;
- think visually in the **small/medium shallow-water cargo-schuit family**;
- use **kromsteven** or **krabschuit** only as a researched analogue or if the scene deliberately chooses a plausible fictional type;
- never phrase either type as if recovered from the De Hont act.

Do **not** upgrade to a large prestigious ocean-going ship merely to make the trade feel important. Cornelis' network value lies in reliable regional movement, not in owning a fleet.

# How much beer could one movement involve?

## Direct Goese scale anchor: 52 tons, 1589

A separate Goese act from **11 April 1589** is unusually valuable. Goese schipper **Jan de Wulf** testified that about a month earlier he had taken **52 tons of English beer** as freight from the vessel of a Veere skipper at the head of Goes, intending to carry them to Zierikzee, where the beer was sold.

Primary locator: `RAZE 2039, fol. 80, 11-4-1589`.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.GOES.BEER_FREIGHT.52_TONNES.1589.001`.

This proves that, in precisely this regional system and year:

`beer shipment in tens of tons -> transshipment at Goes -> regional onward carriage`

was real.

### What this allows in prose

A shipment involving **dozens of beer casks/tons** no longer needs to be treated as speculative atmosphere. It has a direct Goese 1589 comparator.

### What it does not allow

Never write:

- De Hont's ship held 52 tons;
- 52 tons was the standard load;
- De Hont carried this particular English beer;
- one `ton` equals one exact universal litre value;
- Jan de Wulf's ship type equals De Hont's.

## Metrological visualisation only

A scholarly reconstruction gives the **Holland beer ton around 1590** as approximately **154 litres**, while also noting that the **Delft beer ton in 1578 was smaller than the Amsterdam ton**.

Source claims:

- `SC.HIST.HOLLAND.BEER_TON.1590.154L.001`
- `SC.HIST.DELFT.BEER_TON.SMALLER_THAN_AMSTERDAM.1578.001`

If 154 L is used merely as a Holland-scale proxy, 52 tons would be about **8,008 litres**. That is a derived visualization, not a recovered volume for the 1589 consignment and certainly not De Hont's capacity.

**Scene-safe rule:** preserve `tonnen bier` in the historical layer; use litre conversion only in research notes or sensory planning.

# Delft beer and Zeeland: is the route itself plausible?

Yes, at the mechanism level.

## Period comparator, 1540s

Archival-description material for Heenvliet states that small one-sail fishing schuiten also worked inland routes and that the Heenvliet–Delft route was frequently sailed because most local beer came from Delft breweries. A 1541 incident describes a skipper waiting for flood tide before entering harbour.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.DELFT.BEER_SMALL_SCHUIT_ROUTE.1541.001`.

This does not prove Delft–Goes duration or De Hont's vessel, but it establishes recurring Delft-beer water traffic by small craft in Cornelis' adult world.

## Early-modern descriptive source

The 1612 Dutch edition of Guicciardini's Delft description says that Delft beer was carried particularly to Zeeland. An explicitly marked later addition describes wider regional distribution, including Zeeland, with regional `biersteeckers`.

Source claim: `SC.HIST.DELFT.BEER_TO_ZEELAND.GUICCIARDINI.1612.001`.

Because the consulted edition is 1612 and was expanded from the 1567 original, do not silently date the added biersteker wording to 1567.

## Near-contemporary local confirmation, 1609

RAZE 2043 shows Delft beer moving through a named biersteker into the Goes/South-Beveland hinterland, including credit sales and ton-by-ton delivery. It also shows a Delft brewer using Goese shipping/agency connections for debt collection.

Source claims:

- `SC.NEAR.GOES.DELFT_BEER.BIERSTEKER_CREDIT.1609.001`
- `SC.NEAR.GOES.DELFT_BREWER.SHIPPER_DEBT_NETWORK.1609.001`

These later records closely match the **mechanism family** implied by Horen–De Hont without determining the missing 1589 terms.

# How long does Delft–Goes or Goes–Antwerp take?

## Current answer: exact duration OPEN

No direct sixteenth-century De Hont itinerary or trustworthy Delft–Goes duration has been recovered in this research pass.

Therefore the Story Bible must **not** create a fake timetable such as `Delft -> Goes = 18 hours` or `Goes -> Antwerp = one day`.

What is historically safe is the operating logic:

- shallow water and channels matter;
- flood/ebb windows matter;
- wind matters;
- waiting at anchor or for water is normal;
- loading, transshipment and unloading consume time;
- war, inspection or political control can add delay in later periods.

The 1541 Heenvliet example explicitly shows a small-craft skipper waiting for the flood tide to enter harbour. The 1586 Scheldt crabschuijt accident demonstrates sudden weather risk.

### Scene rule

Think in **tides and days, not clock-hours**.

A scene may say that a departure waits on water, wind or light. It may allow arrival to slip by a tide or a day. It should not quote a fixed historical transit duration unless a route-specific source is later found.

The Antwerp extension remains a separate fictionally authorized route for Cornelis; its duration is not established by the 1589 De Hont act.

# Who bears the financial risk?

## Operational risk is historical; contractual allocation is OPEN

This distinction matters.

### Historically visible operational risks

The source world contains:

- a krabschuit capsized by a squall on the Scheldt in 1586;
- transshipment from one skipper's vessel into another at Goes in the 52-ton beer act of 1589;
- near-contemporary evidence of a Goese vessel capsizing on the short Goes–Arnemuiden leg in 1608;
- credit, obligations, sureties and debt collection throughout the beer-distribution system.

So leakage, breakage, weather, delay, failed delivery, bad debt and cask-return problems are all materially credible scene pressures.

### What the Horen–De Hont extract does not tell us

The 1589 extract ends its summary with the equivalent of `following conditions`, but those conditions are not reproduced.

Therefore we do **not** know from the consulted extract:

- whether De Hont owned the beer;
- whether a Delft brewer owned it until delivery;
- whether Horen bought on arrival;
- whether Horen sold on commission;
- who paid freight;
- who bore loss before unloading;
- how leakage or souring was handled;
- who owned/returned empty casks;
- whether Horen was exclusive;
- how or when payment was due.

### Authoring rule

Until the underlying folio 86 conditions are recovered, scenes may dramatize **uncertainty and negotiation** but may not present one risk split as historical law or as the recovered De Hont contract.

If the plot needs a specific Cornelis–De Hont arrangement before 1569, that arrangement must be labeled **novel canon**, not borrowed falsely from Horen's 1589 act.

# Cornelis' death does not erase the network

When Cornelis dies in 1569, several things can survive him even if his personal enterprise does not:

- knowledge of customers and suppliers;
- outstanding accounts;
- cask-return obligations;
- family-brewery supply relationships;
- route knowledge;
- a schipper who knows where goods used to go;
- credit reputations and introductions.

War, repression and family disruption mean this does **not** have to become one clean sale of a going concern.

Preferred model:

`fragmentation -> temporary family/creditor handling -> wartime disruption -> later consolidation by another biersteker`

# Michiel Michielsz Horen as later successor

Horen is the later Goese biersteker who absorbs or continues part of the commercial function associated in fiction with Cornelis.

Historically:

- in 1588 he is described as formerly resident in Aardenburg and then a poorter of Goes;
- in 1589 he agrees to be biersteker for beers De Hont brings to Goes;
- in 1593 he is again named as a biersteker in South-Beveland beer evidence.

Therefore do not write that Horen stood beside Cornelis' deathbed in 1569 or immediately bought his business. Current evidence does not place him in Goes then.

Instead, Horen's role is **eventual succession of function**. By the late 1580s, part of the local-distribution role once associated with Cornelis has found another Goese biersteker, while the same historically named schipper can still be present.

This makes the 1589 agreement a subtle afterlife of Cornelis' commercial world without falsifying the act's direction.

# Story value

## For Claes

If Claes later encounters De Hont, hears his name, or encounters Horen's business, recognition can work without exposition: a man, cask habit, freight phrase, unloading practice or credit convention survives his father.

This can produce a concrete version of a recurring Claes theme:

> people disappear; systems, debts, routes and objects keep moving.

## For the clandestine-paper line

De Hont must not automatically become a professional smuggler. His value is precisely that his traffic is legitimate.

If Cornelis later uses ordinary commercial routes for sensitive letters/books, De Hont's involvement must be scene-specific:

- he may knowingly help;
- he may know only part of the truth;
- he may simply carry a legitimate-looking consignment.

Do not make the 1589 historical agreement retroactively evidence of clandestine activity.

# Scene-ready continuity markers

Useful repeated details can include:

- De Hont's way of counting or marking casks;
- a repeated freight phrase or account convention;
- waiting on a tide before moving;
- the physical labour of rolling/hoisting casks at transshipment;
- knowledge of the same Goese unloading point;
- a surviving supplier or tapper name;
- De Hont correcting someone because Cornelis used to do it differently;
- years later, Horen using a route or habit that Claes associates with his father.

These are authoring devices, not historical claims unless separately sourced.

# Never write as historical fact

- that Aernouldt de Hont is documented with Cornelis before 1569;
- that De Hont is proven to have sailed to Antwerp before 1569;
- that Horen hired De Hont to transport beer owned by Horen;
- that Horen directly purchased Cornelis' business in 1569;
- that Horen lived in Goes in 1569;
- that the 1589 agreement legally continues a Nissepat contract;
- that De Hont sailed a kromstevenschuit or krabschuit;
- that De Hont's vessel carried 52 beer tons;
- that the exact 1589 contractual conditions or risk allocation are known;
- that Delft–Goes or Goes–Antwerp had a fixed recovered transit time;
- that `van Delft` is De Hont's surname — the source says **Aernouldt de Hont, schipper van Delft**.
