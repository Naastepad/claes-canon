# Cornelis economic position — 1542–1568

**Domain:** `WORLD.CORNELIS_ECONOMIC_POSITION_1542_1568`  
**Status:** ACTIVE AUTHORING DOMAIN  
**Authority:** subordinate to current `canon/` decisions and explicit evidence boundaries  
**Core character:** `ENT.PERSON.CORNELIS`

This dossier calibrates **what Cornelis' business actually is, how large it feels, what status it gives him in Goes, and what the 1554 fire destroys economically**.

It deliberately separates:

1. historical context and analogues;
2. explicit novel canon;
3. working numerical calibration.

The numerical bands below are not claimed as an archival balance sheet of a real Cornelis Nissepat.

---

## 1. Social position before the fire

Cornelis is a **well-to-do to very well-to-do poorter**, not a wage-dependent craftsman and not an Antwerp-scale merchant magnate.

In Goes he should read as a man who:

- owns productive capital;
- buys and sells in quantity rather than only at retail;
- can give and receive trade credit;
- deals directly with schippers, brewers, tappers and other merchants;
- can have workers or carriers move goods without doing all heavy labour himself;
- has commercial contacts outside Goes;
- is known to civic measuring/tax institutions;
- can participate naturally in the schutterij/rederijker/poorter social world.

But do **not** automatically make him:

- a burgomaster or schepen;
- a regent;
- one of the richest men of Goes;
- a great international merchant with his own fleet.

The useful category is:

> **substantial urban merchant-poorter with productive assets and regional/international reach.**

---

## 2. His pre-1554 portfolio

Cornelis' livelihood is intentionally diversified.

### A. Biersteker / beer merchant-distributor

Cornelis does not brew his own beer as his core occupation. He operates on the commercial side of the chain:

`family / outside brewery -> cask -> carrier/schipper -> Cornelis -> store/account -> tapper/inn/customer -> settlement`

His advantage within the Nissepat family beer network is not just a cheap cask price. It can include:

- **preferential purchase price**;
- **supplier credit / later settlement**;
- priority access to suitable quantities or batches;
- more flexible cask-return/loss handling;
- family tolerance for uneven cashflow;
- reliable access when an outsider might have to pay immediately.

This matters because supplier credit reduces the amount of cash Cornelis must lock up in inventory.

### Authoring scale

Use roughly **200–350 tons of beer per year** as the internal pre-fire throughput range.

This is a scale-control tool, not a historical statistic.

It implies a business large enough to require recurring deliveries, accounts and customers, but not a monopoly.

### Market profile

Primary market:

- Goes;
- South Beveland villages;
- herbergen/tappers;
- households or institutions where source/context permits;
- nearby Zeeland commercial traffic.

Secondary/external trade:

- selected shipments or sales beyond the immediate region;
- Antwerp can occur, but ordinary Goese beer is **not** made the central Antwerp export commodity.

For Cornelis, Antwerp is commercially more important through **salt, mixed trade, contacts and later print traffic** than through a fictional mass export of Goese beer.

---

## 3. Salt is the main productive-capital arm

Before 18 May 1554 Cornelis owns a working **saltwork / zoutkeet**.

For authoring physical scale, imagine approximately **2–3 pans**: substantial enough to be meaningful productive capital, not so large that Cornelis becomes a dominant Goese industrialist.

This is a working reconstruction only.

The salt arm gives him:

- fixed productive infrastructure;
- fuel requirements;
- labour requirements;
- stock awaiting sale;
- future production already commercially meaningful;
- bulk freight needs;
- a commodity that naturally connects Goes to larger external markets.

### Antwerp

In novel canon **Antwerp is one important external destination for Cornelis' salt**, not the only destination.

That makes the Goes–Antwerp relationship economically native before clandestine literature enters the story.

Cornelis does not travel to Antwerp merely because the plot needs a secret network. He already has a reason to know:

- merchants;
- factors;
- storage or delivery points;
- schippers;
- payment and credit practices;
- what can travel with an ordinary commercial consignment.

---

## 4. The preferred schipper

Cornelis has a recurring, trusted/preferred relationship with a **schipper** who can carry goods between the Goese/Zeeland network and outside destinations, especially Antwerp.

Do **not** yet fix:

- the person's name;
- whether the person is historical or wholly fictional;
- vessel type;
- ownership/co-ownership;
- fixed sailing schedule;
- whether one schipper handles both salt and beer;
- a later beurtvaart monopoly.

These remain research questions.

The relationship is more important than ship ownership.

Cornelis' competence lies in being able to say, in effect:

> I know who can move this, when, at what risk, and who will receive it at the other end.

That same legitimate infrastructure can later become a vulnerability when letters, books or people move through it.

### Historical analogue

A late-sixteenth-century Goese case is directly useful as mechanism: the Goese biersteker **Michiel Horen** made a contract with Delft schipper **Aernouldt de Hont** concerning beer transport in 1589. This is later than Cornelis but demonstrates that `Goese biersteker <-> named external schipper <-> beer transport contract` is a real local economic pattern.

## 5. Capital calibration — spring 1554

The model should not imagine Cornelis with thousands of guilders in a chest.

His wealth is distributed across productive equipment, stock, receivables, casks, goods in motion and obligations.

### Working balance-sheet band

| Component | Authoring range / function |
|---|---:|
| saltwork + pans / productive rights and equipment | roughly 700–1,000 gld |
| salt/fuel and related productive stock | roughly 250–450 gld |
| beer/casks/distribution working assets | roughly 200–350 gld |
| receivables / trade credit owed to Cornelis | roughly 400–600 gld |
| storage/tools/mixed commercial goods | roughly 200–350 gld |
| cash / immediately available money | roughly 150–300 gld |
| **gross controlled assets** | **roughly 2,500–3,500 gld** |
| supplier debt / commercial obligations | **roughly 500–900 gld** |
| **net authoring position** | **roughly 1,800–2,800 gld** |

**Default internal calibration:** about **2,300 guilders net** immediately before the fire.

These values are **fictional economic calibration**, not historical valuation evidence.

### How to show this in prose

Usually do not state `Cornelis is worth 2,300 guilders`.

Show it as:

- a supplier who accepts delayed settlement;
- a salt stock that represents months of money;
- a customer account still unpaid;
- several casks expected back;
- a schipper owed freight;
- productive equipment that cannot simply be converted to cash;
- the difference between being wealthy and being liquid.

---

## 6. Why this status is plausible rather than extravagant

The local economic anchors show that productive commercial assets could reach large values.

A complete Goese brewery with associated equipment was valued in 1582 at no more than **4,500 guilders**. That is later and not a direct 1554 price comparator, but it shows that a substantial urban production business could embody capital on a scale of thousands of guilders.

The local salt evidence describes salt production as profitable/desirable property and shows salt stock plus future production being directly sold to a schipper.

Cornelis' reconstructed wealth is therefore deliberately set:

- **below** ownership of a major complete brewery-scale concern;
- **well above** dependence on daily wages;
- high enough that destruction of one productive arm creates a genuine credit crisis.

---

## 7. 18 May 1554 — economic catastrophe and family catastrophe happen together

The fire destroys several different forms of security at once.

### Household loss — already canon

Cornelis loses:

- Tanneken;
- Jan, the younger son;
- the unborn child;
- the family's use of the Nieuwstraat home.

The Nieuwstraat property itself remains canonically the asset of Cornelis' father, Claes Jacobsz. Nissepat, so it is **not** silently booked as Cornelis' own real-estate loss.

### Cornelis' business loss

The fire destroys Cornelis' **saltwork**.

The economic consequence includes more than the building:

- productive capacity disappears;
- pans/equipment are lost or rendered unusable;
- current salt/fuel stock can be lost;
- expected future output vanishes;
- contracts and customers still exist even when the production asset is gone;
- some customers/debtors in the burned city may themselves be unable to pay;
- Cornelis' own supplier/freight obligations do not automatically disappear.

### Do not silently add more destruction

Unless separately decided, do **not** assume that the fire also destroys:

- a specific Turfkade warehouse;
- every beer cask he owns;
- all account books;
- all receivables;
- all trade stock;
- the Nissepat family brewery.

The family brewery survives as the crucial remaining economic lifeline.

## 8. Post-fire numerical calibration

For scene logic, use approximately:

| Position | Authoring range |
|---|---:|
| immediate business/economic loss | **1,200–1,700 gld** |
| post-fire net position | **600–1,000 gld** |
| genuinely liquid resources | **200–400 gld** |
| approximate decline in net economic position | **60–70%** |

Again: these are internal novel calculations, not archival facts.

The key economic sentence is:

> **A solvent, substantial merchant suddenly becomes undercapitalized and illiquid.**

That is different from becoming a pauper.

---

## 9. Why the surviving brewery matters

The family brewery survives the 1554 fire.

That means Cornelis still has access to one functioning commercial chain:

`family production -> preferential terms / supplier credit -> Cornelis -> customers -> later settlement`

Before the fire he can finance a meaningful part of his own commercial cycle.

After the fire he increasingly needs the family network to finance inventory and carry risk.

His work therefore shifts from:

> **owner-merchant with productive capital**

more toward:

> **network merchant / commission trader / biersteker using other people's capital and stock.**

This is not failure. It is a different economic position.

---

## 10. Wealth falls faster than status

The social consequence should not be written as an instant class collapse.

Cornelis remains:

- a poorter;
- a Nissepat;
- someone who has previously paid and been paid;
- someone merchants and schippers know;
- someone with a father/family network;
- someone who knows routes, measures, customers and settlements.

His **reputation and access** therefore decay more slowly than his actual net worth.

This explains how he can still:

- obtain credit;
- conduct trade;
- reach Antwerp;
- find a workable education route for Claes;
- later become useful in a clandestine logistics network;

while no longer being able to fund life as if 1554 had never happened.

A useful authoring contrast is:

> **before 1554 he mostly owns things; after 1554 he increasingly owns knowledge of people, routes and obligations.**

---

## 11. Direct effect on Claes' education

The existing post-fire father-son canon now has an economic mechanism.

The original Zierikzee plan does not become impossible because Cornelis has zero money.

It becomes irresponsible because:

- productive capital has been destroyed;
- liquid reserves are thin;
- debts and obligations remain;
- future income is less secure;
- Cornelis must rebuild his own livelihood;
- grandfather Claes Jacobsz has also lost the Nieuwstraat property asset.

Reimerswaal becomes the cheaper viable route obtained through relationship/network capital.

This makes sending Claes away simultaneously:

- an economic necessity;
- an educational sacrifice;
- an act of care;
- a second experienced abandonment for Claes after the deaths in the fire.

---

## 12. Antwerp after the fire

Cornelis' access to Antwerp remains plausible even when he is no longer as wealthy.

Why?

Because access depends partly on:

- established commercial contacts;
- a trusted shipper/carrier relationship;
- previous salt trade;
- knowledge of merchants and delivery nodes;
- family and credit reputation;
- the ability to act as intermediary rather than principal investor.

His Antwerp connection can therefore survive the destruction of the saltwork.

The **economic route precedes the clandestine route**.

This is critical for later book traffic: forbidden literature does not give Cornelis his network; it exploits a network he already has.

## 14. Never write

- Cornelis as a brewer because the family brewery supplies him;
- the 200–350-ton band as a discovered historical Nissepat turnover;
- the 2,300-guilder default as a historical inventory;
- Antwerp as the destination of every beer or salt shipment;
- Cornelis as owner of a private ship unless separately decided;
- the preferred schipper's identity before research/author decision fixes it;
- the fire as destroying the Nissepad family brewery;
- grandfather's Nieuwstraat house as Cornelis' personal capital asset;
- `the fire made him poor` without the more precise distinction between wealth, liquidity, status and network capital;
- a monetary valuation of Tanneken, Jan or the unborn child.
