## 2. Core rule: death becomes readable

In Claes' world an execution is not simply the state killing a criminal. It is a public or deliberately hidden act in which body, soul, authority, spectators and memory compete over meaning.

The execution arc moves through three scripts:

1. **Catholic civic ars moriendi** — the condemned dies as sinner within a shared order: confession, priest, cross, repentance, prayer, compassio from the public, restoration of civic and divine order.
2. **Reformation martyr script** — the condemned refuses the old script: no recantation, no Catholic consolation as submission, no final confession in the expected form; instead there may be psalm-singing, letters, admonitions, steadfastness, witness and community memory.
3. **Cornelis' third script** — neither ordinary repentant criminal nor loud martyr. He dies in protective silence: no names, no full explanation, no recovery of the father-son rupture. His death becomes moral inheritance rather than doctrinal triumph.

**Canon formula:**

> Claes grows up seeing execution as a Catholic ritual of order. He comes of age when heresy executions fracture that ritual. He later understands that bodies can be burned, mouths closed and deaths hidden, but texts, letters, songs, objects and codes can still carry witness.

## 3. Casteels layer: execution crisis before and during the Revolt

Use Casteels as the principal analytical frame for execution culture.

Relevant source-derived principles:

- Executions worked only if authorities, condemned person and spectators followed a shared script.
- The expected Christian response of spectators was not simple cruelty but compassion ordered by the ritual.
- Heresy executions became unstable because dissident communities used ordinary spectator roles — comforting, kissing, singing, encouraging, gathering, witnessing — to reverse the meaning of the execution.
- Antwerp between 1557 and 1564 is central: the beul executed 160 people; 83 were for crimes in fide; 55 heresy victims were secretly drowned in Het Steen. These were not unknown deaths, but closed-access deaths, often at night, designed to prevent interference.
- Fabritius' execution on 4 October 1564 is the best candidate for the storybible's existing `NI.EVENT.SECURITY_BREAK.1564.001`: a public execution that authorities tried to control but spectators, psalms, stones and rescue attempts turned into a political-religious crisis.
- After 1564, local governments became increasingly reluctant to execute heretics publicly. The prosecution of heresy became entangled with urban privilege, central authority, noble opposition, popular disorder and the coming Wonderjaar.
- After 1572, rebel justice and Geuzen violence invert the same repertoire: mock trials, hanging clergy, ritual humiliation and Catholic counter-martyrdom show that the new order is not morally pure.

This prevents the novel from becoming a simple line from Catholic oppression to Protestant liberation. Claes must see that every regime wants to control death's meaning.

## 4. Martelaarsbron layer: voice after body

Use the Martelaarsspiegel / doopsgezinde martelaars tradition as a memory source, not as neutral legal record.

Functions for the novel:

- It preserves names, family chains, last words, letters, songs, prison scenes and object relics.
- It turns executions into transferable memory: what the government meant as punishment becomes testimony.
- It supplies the motif chain **body -> fire/water -> ash -> letter/song/relic -> code**.
- It is especially useful for Maeyken Wens and Hans Bret: the mouth is controlled by the tongschroef, but voice survives as letter, object and community memory.

Guardrail: when using Braght or martelaarscompilations, label them as martyrological/herinneringsbron. Where modern scholarship corrects dates, methods or names, keep the correction visible.

## 5. Life-phase placement

### I. Goes before 1554 — Catholic baseline

Claes' first understanding of execution belongs to the Catholic civic order. Ordinary criminals — thieves, murderers, violent offenders, bandits — can be executed harshly, but the ideal death remains intelligible: priest, confession, cross, repentance, prayer, crowd compassion. The body is punished but the soul may still be ordered toward mercy.

This baseline matters because later heresy deaths are shocking precisely because they refuse or disrupt the expected script.

### II. 1554–1561 — Reimerswaal and hearsay after personal loss

After the Goes fire and the move to Reimerswaal, Claes receives execution news as rumor, school talk, merchant talk, religious warning and traveling memory. Pleuntgen van der Goes is especially important: a Goese name disappears in Antwerps water, not on a visible Goese market.

### III. 1561–1564 — Antwerp dodenruis

From the Landjuweel onward Antwerp surrounds Claes with a hidden execution economy: Het Steen, closed doors, barrels/tubs, night, bodies, rumor, songs, market executions and book networks. He does not need to see every death. He must feel the density of names.

### IV. 1563 — Jan Grendel in Goes

Jan Jansz. Grendel is the local Goese faith-death anchor. With the canonical 1542 birth, Claes is about twenty. This is not a small child's first sight but a young man's confrontation with the fact that Goes itself can burn a believer.

### V. 4 October 1564 — Fabritius and the security break

Fabritius is the strongest historical candidate for `NI.EVENT.SECURITY_BREAK.1564.001`. The event should be written as the moment at which the old practical plan fails: Cornelis can no longer safely receive, hold or transmit the direct key. Claes' existing memoriaal is therefore turned into a blind recovery carrier after the break.

Cornelis need not throw stones, sing, help or be arrested in 1564. The point is systemic: the death ritual itself has become a communication crisis.

### VI. 1568/1569 — Cornelis' execution witnessed by Claes

Cornelis does not die because he carries the memoriaal. He does not carry it. He dies because he is exposed as a low-protection logistics node: routes, vaten, storage, papers, contacts, possibly psalm/lied/libel/prent traffic, and refusal to name others.

The execution should place him between scripts:

- the authorities offer or stage the old Catholic death script;
- the Reformation has made refusal readable as witness;
- Cornelis refuses names rather than making a loud doctrinal performance.

Claes witnesses the execution. The fixed story effect is not doctrinal conversion but moral trauma: he sees that silence can protect and destroy at once.

### VII. 1570 — recovery and moral burden

The recovery sequence is not puzzle entertainment. It becomes intelligible only because Claes has learned what execution culture does: it destroys the body, controls speech, hides access, and forces truth to survive through indirect carriers. The memoriaal's hidden layer and the later Las Casas recovery are therefore morally continuous with letters, songs, martyr books and relics.

### VIII. 1572–1577 — mirror and inversion

Later rebel violence, Catholic counter-martyrs, Maeyken Wens and Hans Bret deepen the theme. The new order can also use death as theater; tongschroeven and relics show that attempts to silence witnesses often create stronger memory.

## 8. Cornelis: plausible death model

Cornelis' death must not depend on him carrying the memoriaal. The memoriaal is already with Claes and later secretly prepared.

Plausible charge cluster:

- forbidden books, psalm leaves, libels, songs, anti-inquisition or sedition papers;
- logistical distribution through barrels, freight, storage, shipping and Goese/Antwerp links;
- dangerous contacts rather than intellectual authorship;
- refusal to give names;
- after 1566/1567, reclassification from book/transport matter to sedition, heresy assistance and network participation.

Why Cornelis can die while Plantin/Silvius do not:

- Plantin/Silvius have professional status, patrons, printshop structures, plausible deniability, political/economic usefulness and routes to delay or survive investigation.
- Cornelis is a practical, lower-protection carrier. The physical route, not authorship, incriminates him.
- He cannot explain without endangering others and cannot fully betray the recovery system because he never knows the full key.

Preferred story effect:

> Cornelis dies because he is the route that can still name other routes. His silence protects others and leaves Claes with an inheritance that is incomplete by design.

## 9. Motif integration

Add or activate these motifs when writing execution-related scenes:

- **Schavot / plein** — public order that can become contested theater.
- **Mond / stem / stilte** — confession, psalm, refusal, tongschroef, letter, silence.
- **Vat / kuip / water / Steen** — hidden execution and Cornelis' trade-world darkened by death practice.
- **Kruis / biecht / weigering** — old script versus witness script.
- **As / reliek / ijzer** — destroyed body, surviving object.
- **Carrier remains / meaning changes** — same structural motif as music, church interior, memoriaal and code.
- **Fire/water** — historical material death first; alchemical resonance second.

## 10. Writing guardrails

- Do not turn Claes into an anachronistic anti-Catholic observer. He begins within Catholic forms of order.
- Do not make all Protestant or doopsgezinde deaths identical; distinguish Reformed public psalm/sermon networks from Anabaptist nonviolence and martyr-letter tradition.
- Do not make Cornelis a full Protestant preacher unless separately decided. His strongest role is carrier, protector, father and silent node.
- Do not give Cornelis the full recovery key.
- Do not have Claes know Las Casas or the hidden recovery chain before 1570.
- Do not overuse spectacle. The strongest scenes are not the most graphic ones but those in which Claes understands that meaning has shifted.
- Keep public execution, secret drowning, torture death, corpse display, relic-memory and later martyrology as distinct forms.
