## Dependency rule
**source → HIST.EVENT → historical/world consequence → possible Narrative Instance / Story Claim**

Historical relevance never creates fictional Claes participation by itself.

## Scene rule
For every scene between 1540 and 1605, test at minimum:
1. political control and jurisdiction;
2. public worship versus private confession;
3. military status: front, garrison, siege zone, supply route, maritime corridor or rear area;
4. shipping/tides and transport where relevant;
5. provisioning, taxation, billets, labour and prices where sources permit;
6. state of church/public space, guilds, confraternities and rederijkers;
7. print/books/censorship and migrant networks;
8. sensory consequences that an inhabitant could actually perceive;
9. information status: what is seen, heard, rumoured, read, verified or merely repeated;
10. layered identity: town/province/corporation/confession/dynastic allegiance/fatherland language must not be collapsed into one modern national label;
11. later memory: distinguish event, personal perception, retelling and public commemoration.

## Information ecology guardrail
For the 1560s–1570s, oral circulation is normally the first carrier of current news. Pamphlets and prints can be important but may follow oral reports by days or weeks. False rumours may still have real historical consequences. Scene narration must therefore distinguish a character's information state from historical fact.

## Current completion boundary
The **macro historical sequence relevant to the Claes project is populated from 1540 through 1605** in the parent timeline. This means the principal political, military, confessional and civic transformations that can materially change Claes' scene world are represented. It does **not** mean that every skirmish, ordinance, office-holder or local daily-life variable has been catalogued.

Remaining research is explicitly microhistorical, especially:
- Goes 1572 civilian/guild/church/provisioning experience;
- exact Goese implementation of the 1578 religious shift;
- Reimerswaal's interaction of war, flood/erosion, demography, trade and church life;
- annual/seasonal Zeeland data on taxation, billeting, provisioning, disease, prices and shipping.

## Source discipline
Use `VERIFIED`, `SUPPORTED` and open/research statuses exactly as declared in the history files. General Low Countries evidence may explain mechanisms, but exact local implementation in Goes or Reimerswaal requires local support.

The current public-opinion/identity layer is grounded in targeted review of Robert Stein & Judith Pollmann (eds.), *Networks, Regions and Nations* (2010), and Judith Pollmann & Andrew Spicer (eds.), *Public Opinion and Changing Identities in the Early Modern Netherlands* (2007). These are not represented as integral cover-to-cover readings.
