## Historical anchors

- **22 March 1577:** Goes/Zuid-Beveland concludes the Satisfactie with William of Orange. The settlement brings the city under Orange's stadholder authority on negotiated terms while protecting Roman Catholic religious practice and limiting unilateral political/military imposition.
- Goes is therefore **not** to be written as instantly Reformed in March 1577.
- **1578:** wider religious-political radicalisation destabilizes Catholic-only or Catholic-protective settlement arrangements. Reformed preaching and pressure expand in satisfactie towns, including Goes in the class-level synthesis already held by this repository.
- **1578:** Orange's own religious-peace policy is argued in terms of coexistence/toleration across confessional difference. That line must remain historically distinguishable from later Calvinist monopolisation or local coercive change.
- **January 1574:** the naval battle near Reimerswaal contributes to Middelburg's surrender the following month. This is separate from the **1573 burning of Reimerswaal**, which Claes now canonically witnesses from a boat.

## Storybible use

For Goes, the safe historical sequence is:

**royalist siege city (1572) → negotiated political transfer with Catholic protection (22 March 1577) → growing confessional pressure → Reformed expansion in 1578 → contested public religious space.**

Do not equate any single transition with instant population-wide conversion.

Use `WORLD.RELIGIOUS_SPACE.SENSORY_CHURCH` for embodied church scenes. The church may remain materially recognizable while:
- authorised speakers change;
- preaching changes;
- processions/ritual visibility changes;
- access and permitted worship change;
- familiar civic-religious signs acquire new political meaning.

This gives the *sinne* motif a historically grounded transformation of familiar space.

## The strategic-toleration possibility — FICTION OPEN, not historical fact

A new authorial possibility is deliberately preserved without closure:

> Claes may reason that Goes can be brought toward the Reformed side more effectively if Catholic practice is initially guaranteed, political resistance is reduced, and the old confession is only later displaced from everyday public life.

If adopted, this is **Claes' fictional strategic reasoning**.

It must not be back-projected as the historical purpose of the Satisfactie.

Four models remain available:

1. **Observer:** Claes merely understands the mechanism after the fact.
2. **Peripheral hint:** Claes offers a limited insight that enters a larger discussion.
3. **Instrumental strategist:** Claes deliberately advocates staged toleration as a path to later confessional displacement.
4. **Moral opponent:** Claes encounters the instrumental strategy and resists it.

### If Model 3 is selected

The scene must not celebrate instrumental toleration as neutral cleverness. It would create a real moral problem:

- conscience becomes a tool of statecraft;
- protection becomes temporary camouflage;
- Claes risks reproducing the coercive logic he hates, only with reversed confessional direction;
- revenge against the structures associated with Cornelis' death may contaminate his judgement;
- his knowledge of Goes makes him effective precisely where personal injury makes him least neutral.

Possible revenge motive remains **OPEN**, not canon.

## Counterforce: Orange's religious-peace line

The historical context gives Claes a meaningful counterposition.

Orange's 1578 religious-peace argument can function as:
- principled restraint;
- pragmatic coalition-building;
- or a sincere belief that confessions must coexist.

The story should not flatten this into one motive without evidence.

If Claes advocates tactical toleration followed by exclusion, he may therefore be **more manipulative than Orange on this point**. That is potentially valuable because it makes Claes' intelligence morally dangerous rather than automatically wise.
